ASR – Adaptive Secure Recovery
Adaptive Secure Recovery Standard for Cryptocurrency Accounts

**ASR Protocol Version: 1.0.0**
___
___
# Introduction

One of the most significant challenges in the cryptocurrency ecosystem is the secure storage and safeguarding of recovery phrases. Standard protocols such as BIP-39, despite their simplicity and widespread adoption, have designed recovery as a fully offline process. As a result, conventional methods lack any independent cryptographic layer or active defense mechanisms, and rely solely on the recovery phrase itself. This design introduces a fundamental vulnerability: if the Seed phrase is compromised, the user's entire assets become accessible and transferable without any security barrier, without network oversight, and without any rate limiting.

In this context, the ASR (Adaptive Secure Recovery) standard is introduced to eliminate this fundamental weakness. In addition to strengthening the recovery phrase itself — by increasing the entropy of each unit to 13 bits and expanding the number range to 8192 states — ASR fundamentally redefines the recovery process as a network-gated operation. This redefinition operates on two layers:

- User Layer: The user's recovery data is split into two independent components — ASR-REC_Display, which the user maintains as recovery data, and ASR-VAR, which is kept in the user's memory or in a location separate from ASR-REC_Display.
- Entropy Layer: Final_Entropy is split into two independent shares — key_local, which is encoded into the ASR-REC_Main structure, and key_network, which is independently generated by the wallet and stored in a protected form within the network's recovery infrastructure.

The wallet requires both shares, key_local and key_network, to reconstruct Final_Entropy.

The security objective of ASR is that possessing only one of the recovery components is, by itself, insufficient to reconstruct key_local; and reconstructing Final_Entropy is impossible without network access and obtaining key_network from the recovery infrastructure.

ASR is not a cryptographic layer added on top of existing standards; it is a paradigm shift in how digital assets are designed, managed, and protected.

ASR is not limited to expanding the space of recovery values; rather, through a recovery-management-based architecture, it redefines cryptographic credentials by separating local and network shares and applying independent controls throughout the recovery process.

# Rationale

The ASR standard is designed to reduce the risk of unauthorized recovery of cryptocurrency accounts and to prevent offline attacks on recovery data. This standard is intended for scenarios in which part of the recovery data is lost, disclosed, or stolen.

By employing independent protection layers (the user layer and the network layer), the ASR architecture shifts account recovery dependence from a single phrase to a set of complementary data, and moves account security beyond reliance on a single data set.

- The user layer is responsible for preventing unauthorized disclosure of local recovery data (ASR-REC_Display and ASR-VAR).
- The network layer is responsible for preventing offline recovery and limiting unauthorized attempts by an attacker, in scenarios where the attacker has access to only one of the two components of the data required for account recovery.

The recovery process in ASR depends on two components in the user layer:

- ASR-REC_Display, which — depending on the user's choice — has two modes: 12 or 24 four-digit 13-bit numbers; and
- ASR-VAR, which — corresponding to the same prior user choice (12 or 24 four-digit 13-bit numbers) — consists respectively of 12 or 24 uppercase English letters, and acts as the recovery key for the user.

The user must store these two components separately from each other; for example, ASR-VAR may be kept in the user's memory, or in a location separate from ASR-REC_Display (i.e., the numerical data).

The dependence of the recovery process on the two components in the user layer means that, if one of the components is disclosed, completing the recovery process without access to the other component is possible only by guessing the second component or through computational search.

By making the recovery process dependent on the network, the ASR architecture strictly limits an attacker's guessing or computational search via the Rate Limiting policy. The ASR recovery architecture is not a static recovery mechanism. In addition to dependence on the two components in the user layer, it features a network-centric system for detecting unsuccessful and suspicious recovery attempts, and for detecting attempts to recover an account using incomplete recovery data.

When the attacker lacks access to one of the recovery components in the user layer, every attempt by the attacker to recover the account is detected by the network and — under Rate Limiting policies — recorded, reported, and limited.

Dependence on the network makes the following actions by the network possible when one component of the account's recovery data is disclosed:

1. Detection of account recovery attempts in scenarios where the attacker, possessing one of the recovery components in the user layer, is forced to guess or use computational search to obtain the second component.

2. Limiting the attacker's repeated recovery attempts through the Rate Limiting policy.

3. The network, by sending an alert message to the active wallets associated with the same account, informs the owner of the recovery attempt. If the user has previously enabled the delayed account activation option, the attacker will be unable — even with a correct guess and after passing the Rate Limiting policy — to transfer assets before the period specified by the owner has elapsed.

In this architecture, completion of the recovery process first requires, in the user layer, the numerical variable ASR-REC_Display and the lexical variable ASR-VAR, as well as the execution of ASR-CYC cycles for local reconstruction of ASR-REC_Main and derivation of key_local.

Completion of the recovery process — through reconstruction of Final_Entropy — depends on the wallet having access to key_local from the user layer, and access to key_network from the network layer (i.e., the network recovery infrastructure). These two components are necessary and essential for reconstructing Final_Entropy and completing the account recovery process.

Final_Entropy is reconstructed through the XOR operator between key_local and key_network. Consequently, completing the recovery process requires the wallet to have access to both shares — key_local (user layer) and key_network (network layer). The XOR operator in this structure ensures that neither share alone is sufficient to reconstruct Final_Entropy.

key_network is generated independently by the wallet at backup time and is stored in a protected form within the network recovery infrastructure.

At recovery time, the only way for the wallet to obtain key_network is to send both recovery components as ASR-REC_Display_ID and ASR-VAR_ID to the recovery infrastructure, and to pass the network's security controls. These controls include recovery request validation, the Proof-of-Work challenge (PoW), the unified Rate Limiting policy, and protection of key_network. The goal is for repeated or unauthorized recovery attempts — and unauthorized recovery — to be detected and limited by the network. Issuance of recovery authorization takes place only after successful passage through the defined controls.

In addition, the ASR architecture supports alert mechanisms and supplementary protective measures. In the alert system of this architecture, upon detecting an unsuccessful or suspicious recovery attempt, the network recovery infrastructure first limits unauthorized attempts through Rate Limiting policies, and then sends alerts to the active wallets associated with the same account.

After receiving the alert, the account owner may — depending on the level of the identified threat — activate capabilities such as protective delay in account activation, or transfer of assets to a secure account.

# Main Components of the ASR Architecture

- ASR-REC: Recovery Numerical Data
- ASR-VAR: Variable per User Choice
- ASR-CYC: Cycles for Maximum Coverage

## 1. ASR-REC: Recovery Numbers

ASR-REC, within the ASR architecture, is a numerical data type and — based on the user's choice — has two different modes:

- 12-number mode: 12 four-digit 13-bit numbers with an original number range of 0000 to 8191 (a total of 8192 states) — nominal capacity of 156 bits.
- 24-number mode: 24 four-digit 13-bit numbers with an original number range of 0000 to 8191 (a total of 8192 states) — nominal capacity of 312 bits.

Account backup is performed based on one of these two modes, selected by the user.

ASR-REC is displayed to the user as 12 or 24 four-digit numbers, separated by hyphens and prefixed with ASR-REC.

Example of the ASR-REC display format with 12 four-digit numbers:

```
ASR-REC-4735-3238-3956-7095-7337-7032-1291-2345-3227-2728-7285-2690
```

Display Requirements

- English ASCII digits from 0 to 9 only
- No spaces
- Left-to-right display
- Uniform formatting using the hyphen -
- Use of the ASR-REC prefix at the beginning of the string, on the left side of the variable, for data-type identification, reduction of display and input errors, and prevention of homography attacks

This data has two distinct components:

- ASR-REC_Main: the base recovery value that the wallet generates from key_local at account registration time, and which serves as the basis for ASR-CYC computations and integration.
- ASR-REC_Display: the display value that results from integrating ASR-REC_Main with the ASR-CYC output (in the form of mod 8192), and which the user keeps as recovery data.

## 2. ASR-VAR: Variable per User Choice

ASR-VAR, within the ASR architecture, is a lexical data type. Although the letters of this variable are selected and determined by the user in the ASR standard, the number of letter variables is a function of the number of ASR-REC values:

- Whenever the user selects the 12 four-digit number mode for ASR-REC, ASR-VAR has 12 lexical variables.
- And whenever the user selects the 24 four-digit number mode, ASR-VAR has 24 lexical variables.

The letters of ASR-VAR are selected by the user and kept in the user's memory or in a location separate from ASR-REC. ASR-VAR is not stored on the network or in the wallet.

This variable is considered the access key to the assets. Without ASR-VAR, neither the network nor the wallet can map ASR-REC to an account.

Display Requirements

- Uppercase English letters A to Z only
- ASCII encoding only
- No spaces
- Left-to-right display
- Uniform formatting using the hyphen -, with the ASR-VAR prefix at the beginning of the string, on the left side of the variable, for data-type identification, reduction of display and input errors, and prevention of homography attacks
- A maximum of two consecutive repeated letters is allowed

The repetition restriction is applied to prevent the selection of extremely low-entropy variables (e.g., AAAAAAAAAAAA) and is comparable to password policy standards in secure systems.
Allowing up to two consecutive repeated letters ensures linguistic compatibility with natural English language structures.

Example: ASR-VAR display format with 12 lexical variables

```
ASR-VAR-SUNBOYINHOME
```

Each lexical variable of ASR-VAR, after entering the cryptographic cycle specific to this standard (ASR-CYC), is used by default to encrypt four digits of ASR-REC.

For this reason, the number of ASR-VAR variables is not an independent choice; rather, it is a function of the ASR-REC mode selected.
If the user selects the 12-number mode, ASR-VAR has 12 lexical variables;
and if the user selects the 24-number mode, ASR-VAR will have 24 lexical variables.

## 3. ASR-CYC: Cycles for Maximum Coverage

ASR-CYC, or maximum-coverage cycles, is a cyclic process that — through gradual expansion of numerical values and maximum coverage of the range 0000 to 8191 — prevents structural disclosure of ASR-REC_Main.

Security role of these cycles:

If ASR-VAR were directly integrated with ASR-REC_Main (without cycles), each of its letters — which has a value between 1 and 26 — would only create a small range around the original value. In this case, with the disclosure of ASR-REC_Display, an attacker could find the original value in a short time by searching this small range.

The ASR-CYC cycles eliminate this structural weakness:
by executing consecutive additions and applying mod 8192, the final output is dispersed across the entire range 0000 to 8191. As a result, ASR-REC_Display provides the attacker with no structural information about the position of ASR-REC_Main.

ASR-CYC alone does not produce new entropy; rather, it covers a structural vulnerability and spreads the existing entropy of ASR-VAR across the entire ASR-REC structure, thereby expanding the attacker's effective search space to the maximum possible range.

### ASR-CYC is responsible for the following:

- Mapping letters to numbers
- Executing the cycles by summing each variable with the next variable, and the last variable with the first variable (modularly), until the last remaining variable reaches a 9-digit number (≥ 100,000,000)
- Applying mod 8192 to each result to produce the "ASR-CYC final output" in the range 0000–8191
- Modular summation of the ASR-CYC final output with the ASR-REC_Main numbers, in order to obtain ASR-REC_Display and display it to the user

### Letter-to-Number Mapping

The ASR-VAR vocabulary consists exclusively of uppercase English letters A to Z in ASCII.
Each letter maps to a number:
A = 1, B = 2, …, Z = 26

### Cryptographic Cycle Execution:

First, the letter variables are mapped to numbers.
Then, in order, each variable is summed with the next variable, and the last variable is also summed with the first variable.
The summation results are written inside brackets and separated by the - sign, forming the numbers of the variables produced by the first cycle.
To sum the variables in a new cycle, we use the values from the last cycle (i.e., the previous cycle).

With repeated ASR-CYC cycles, numbers with larger digit lengths are produced.

The cycles continue until the last remaining variable reaches a 9-digit number (≥ 100,000,000).

### Recovery Completion Conditions

The account recovery process begins with receiving recovery data from the user. The wallet first receives ASR-REC_Display and then ASR-VAR from the user.

It then uses ASR-VAR to execute the ASR-CYC cycles, obtaining the final output of this stage.

Using ASR-REC_Display and modular subtraction within the range 0000–8191 from the final ASR-CYC output, the wallet reconstructs the ASR-REC_Main value.

### Why is reconstructing ASR-REC_Main alone insufficient to complete recovery?

ASR-REC_Main is only used to reconstruct key_local, and completing the recovery process requires obtaining key_network (the network share) from the network recovery infrastructure. These two shares are both necessary and essential for computing Final_Entropy and recovering the account.

Next, the wallet extracts key_local by decoding ASR-REC_Main. Finally, by combining key_local (the user share) and key_network (the network share, received from the recovery infrastructure) via the XOR operator, it computes Final_Entropy and completes the account recovery process.

To obtain key_network and complete the recovery process, the wallet sends the required information — including ASR-REC_Display_ID and ASR-VAR_ID — to the network recovery infrastructure, and after successfully passing the network's security controls (including Proof-of-Work (PoW) and Rate Limiting), it receives key_network from the network recovery infrastructure.

The final value of Final_Entropy is computed using the following relation, and account recovery is completed:

```
Final_Entropy = key_local XOR key_network
```

### Additional Notes

The ASR-CYC steps are identical in both the 12-number and 24-number modes. In the 24-number mode, to maintain execution order, the number of ASR-VAR variables and the number of ASR-REC positions increases from 12 to 24, and the ASR-CYC cycles are executed over 24 values.

Since ASR-CYC operations consist solely of simple additions, and even at the 9-digit threshold the number of cycles remains limited (approximately 23 to 27 rounds), the computational cost is negligible. Therefore, the 9-digit threshold was chosen so that the uniformity of the output distribution after mod 8192 is maintained at the highest possible level.

In this version, the permitted numerical range for ASR-REC values is 0000 to 8191.

Standard modular rule: mod 8192

Cycle-termination condition: the cycles continue until the last remaining variable reaches a 9-digit number (≥ 100,000,000). Then mod 8192 is applied to each value.

Reason for choosing the 9-digit termination threshold: The larger the range of the final cycle values, the more uniform the distribution produced by mod 8192. With termination at 9 digits, the range length reaches more than 1,220 times the modulus, and the bias is reduced to ~0.08% — effectively zero. By contrast, termination at 6 digits produces a 33% bias, and at 8 digits a 0.66% bias.

# Network-Dependent Account Recovery in ASR: Architectural Constraints and the Impossibility of Offline Recovery

In the ASR standard, the impossibility of offline recovery is not a flaw or implementation limitation, but a design feature. Unlike common standards such as BIP-39, where the entire recovery process is offline and solely dependent on recovery words, ASR, in addition to offline dependence on two values ASR-VAR and ASR-REC_Display, is fundamentally based on receiving data from the network.

The reason for network dependence is the separation of Final_Entropy into two independent shares:

- First, the local share (key_local): which is reconstructed from the user's recovery data and the ASR-REC_Main structure.
- Second, key_network is generated by the wallet and sent to the network. The network stores the key_network sent by the wallet in Core Recovery Key Network Storage and returns a successful storage confirmation message to the wallet.

During the Enrollment phase, the wallet temporarily generates key_network and keeps it in memory to send it in the Enrollment message and receive its successful registration from the network.

After successful completion of Enrollment and receiving the confirmation message, the temporary copy of key_network MUST be erased from the wallet's memory, and the wallet MUST NOT store, retain, display, or provide it to the user. Because:

- key_network is a recovery key belonging to the network, not a user key.
- The user's responsibility is limited to safeguarding and maintaining ASR-REC_Display and ASR-VAR.
- Displaying key_network to the user increases the risk of leakage through user error (screenshots, notes, insecure storage).
- This violates the architectural separation between user data and network data.

Reconstruction of Final_Entropy is only possible through the XOR combination of these two shares:

```
Final_Entropy = key_local XOR key_network
```

Therefore, disclosure of ASR-REC_Display or ASR-VAR alone, even with very high computational power, is not sufficient to reconstruct Final_Entropy; because each of these two components alone does not provide the attacker with the necessary information to complete the recovery process.

The precise security statement of ASR is:

Complete offline recovery, under the threat model defined for ASR, is not possible; because Final_Entropy is composed of key_local and key_network, and key_network can only be obtained through the network-based Recovery path.

Furthermore, disclosure of one of the two components ASR-REC_Display or ASR-VAR alone is not sufficient to reconstruct key_local and obtain key_network from the network, and the attacker needs both components to complete this step.

Supplementary explanations on structure and mechanism

### 1. Dual-Mode Display Structure of ASR for the User

The recovery standard in ASR has two modes: 12-number and 24-number, and backup is performed based on one of these two modes, chosen by the user.

When starting the backup process, the wallet presents both modes to the user so they can choose one. After selecting either the 12-number or 24-number mode, the rest of the generation and recovery process is carried out by the wallet based on that choice.

12-number mode

- ASR-REC = 12 four-digit numbers with 13-bit capacity and original number range: 0000 to 8191, totaling 8192 states
- ASR-VAR = 12 uppercase English letters with range A to Z

```
12 × 13 = 156 bits
Nominal entropy = 156 bits
```

24-number mode

- ASR-REC = 24 four-digit numbers with 13-bit capacity and original number range: 0000 to 8191, totaling 8192 states
- ASR-VAR = 24 uppercase English letters with range A to Z

```
24 × 13 = 312 bits
Nominal entropy = 312 bits
```

The 24-number mode provides a much higher security margin and greater theoretical and numerical resistance against quantum search, based on the NIST standard.

The choice between these two modes (12-number and 24-number, with 13-bit four-digit numbers) MUST be made by the user when starting the recovery process. The wallet cannot display the 12-number mode by default or impose it on the user in any way; rather, wallets MUST display both the 12 and 24-number modes to the user in the first step of backup, so the user can choose one and continue the backup steps based on their choice.

### 2. Removal of Checksum from the ASR Architecture

The ASR standard does not use a Checksum in its core structure.

Checksum is typically used to detect data entry or transmission errors and is not a cryptographic mechanism or a security-enhancing factor. In the ASR architecture, given that the format, length, type, and range of inputs are precisely defined in the standard beforehand, and wallets are required to validate these rules, Checksum is not considered an essential component for ASR security.

Reason for removing Checksum

One of the fundamental principles of ASR is to prevent the recovery process from becoming a problem that can be solved offline (Offline Recovery).

Adding a Checksum creates an additional validation rule that, if the attacker has the necessary data for verification, can allow them to eliminate invalid combinations without going through the complete network recovery process.

ASR has explicit formats and rules for error detection, implementation, and validation. Checksum, as an additional validation constraint, can in certain scenarios further limit the space of acceptable combinations for an attacker. Therefore, ASR refrains from adding unnecessary constraints to its recovery standard.

ASR does not use Checksum to increase the acceptable search space, and Checksum is not considered part of ASR's security entropy.

This decision is more compatible with ASR architectural principles based on tying the recovery process to two variables and the network.

Difference from BIP-39

In BIP-39, Checksum is used for word structure, detecting some input errors, or random changes in the recovery phrase. ASR does not define such a Checksum in its structure. Instead, wallets are required to validate the format, number, character type, and value range of ASR-REC_Display and ASR-VAR according to the standard. This validation detects format errors, but does not claim to detect all typographical errors or valid value transpositions.

No Checksum in ASR-VAR

In the ASR standard, the ASR-VAR input is explicitly limited.

In 12-number mode:

```
ASR-VAR = 12 uppercase English letters
Allowed characters = A-Z
```

In 24-number mode:

```
ASR-VAR = 24 uppercase English letters
Allowed characters = A-Z
```

ASR-compliant wallets MUST check this limitation before continuing the Recovery process (and before computing ASR-CYC).

Therefore, the wallet must not accept inputs such as the following as valid ASR-VAR:

- a-z (lowercase letters)
- 0-9 (digits)
- Persian characters
- Arabic characters
- symbols
- spaces
- other Unicode characters

Since wallets are required to check and validate the type and format of characters entered by the user according to the ASR standard, this largely covers the need for a Checksum to detect user input errors.

ASR-REC Validation without Checksum

The ASR-REC input also has a specific and limited format in the ASR standard.

Each ASR-REC value consists of 12 four-digit 13-bit numbers in the range 0000 to 8191, and the input set must consist solely of allowed digits.

ASR-compliant wallets MUST check the following:

- The number of values is correct;
- Each value has exactly four digits;
- Values are in the range 0000–8191;
- The overall structure of ASR-REC matches the selected mode;
- Values outside the rules defined by the protocol are rejected.
- Entry and display of numbers use English characters.

Therefore, a Checksum is not necessary for detecting structural and format errors in ASR-REC either.

Difference between Checksum and Format Validation

ASR distinguishes between these two concepts:

Format Validation: To check whether the input is structurally compliant with the standard.

```
ASR-VAR → A-Z
ASR-REC → decimal digits 0000–8191
```

Checksum: An additional control value to detect certain errors.

ASR uses Format Validation, but does not define Checksum as a security component or part of the Recovery structure.

Effect on Entropy

With the removal of Checksum, no independent bits are subtracted from the ASR-REC space for Checksum.

Therefore, assuming uniform generation of original values:

12-number mode:

```
12 × 13 = 156 bits
Nominal entropy = 156 bits
```

24-number mode:

```
24 × 13 = 312 bits
Nominal entropy = 312 bits
```

In these calculations, Checksum has no contribution to reducing entropy, because it is essentially not part of the ASR architecture.

Removing Checksum does not mean that ASR has no input validation at all.

On the contrary, ASR performs validation at the level of format, length, range, and data structure, but refrains from adding an unnecessary cryptographic constraint to its Recovery space, especially when such a constraint could be used independently to filter out invalid combinations.

For this reason, ASR lacks a Checksum in both ASR-REC_Main and ASR-VAR, and input validation is performed through explicit standard rules and their mandatory implementation in compliant wallets.

### 3. Relationship between ASR-REC and ASR-VAR Variables

In the twelve-number ASR-REC mode:

- ASR-VAR consists of 12 English letter variables (uppercase A to Z).

In the twenty-four-number ASR-REC mode:

- ASR-VAR consists of 24 English letter variables (uppercase A to Z).

Each character of ASR-VAR, after conversion to a numeric value and passing through the ASR-CYC cycle, produces a four-digit output. This four-digit output is modularly combined with the corresponding value in ASR-REC, and the final result is displayed to the user as ASR-REC_Display.

Therefore:

```
12 ASR-VAR characters ↔ 12 ASR-REC positions
```

and:

```
24 ASR-VAR characters ↔ 24 ASR-REC positions
```

Corresponding to the increase in numeric variables of ASR-REC from 12 four-digit numbers to 24 four-digit numbers, the letter variables of ASR-VAR also increase from 12 letter variables to 24 letter variables. This increase is necessary and essential to preserve the structure, execution steps, and operation of ASR-CYC cycles, without changing the ASR architectural logic.

### 4. One-Way Recovery Identifiers

To prevent direct transmission of ASR-REC_Display and ASR-VAR to the network, ASR uses two one-way identifiers:

ASR-REC_Display Identifier:

```
ASR-REC_Display_ID = SHA-512(Canonical_ASR-REC_Display)
```

ASR-VAR Identifier:

```
ASR-VAR_ID = SHA-512(Canonical_ASR-VAR)
```

Both outputs are SHA-512 and are 64 bytes long.

These identifiers can be converted to a standard representation for transmission and storage.

Important Principle

SHA-512 is a one-way hash function. Computing the identifier from the input data is fast and easy, but directly recovering the input from the hash is computationally infeasible.

However, ASR security should not rely on the secrecy of these two identifiers.

In particular:

ASR-REC_Display_ID and ASR-VAR_ID are not considered secrets.

The main security of ASR is based on the principle that the attacker, as long as they do not simultaneously have access to both ASR-REC_Display and ASR-VAR, will never reach key_network.

key_network is an essential component for reconstructing Final_Entropy and is exclusively held by the network. The network provides key_network to the user only after receiving ASR-REC_Display_ID and ASR-VAR_ID and the successful completion of the Recovery process by the wallet.

Any attempt to bypass the network, without possessing both ASR-REC_Display and ASR-VAR variables, is strictly limited by the Rate Limiting policy.

### 5. NRA — Network Recovery Authorization

NRA (Network Recovery Authorization) means network recovery authorization.

In the ASR architecture, NRA is a control authorization issued by the network's Recovery Service and is used in two main stages:

First — Enrollment Stage

During the Enrollment stage, the wallet sends the information required for account recovery — including the following — along with a signature from the account's Access Key to the network:

- ASR_Version
- Account
- Recovery_Mode
- ASR-REC_Display_ID
- ASR-VAR_ID
- key_network
- KDF_Algorithm
- KDF_Version
- KDF_Parameters
- Derivation_Scheme_Version
- Nonce
- Signing_Key_ID
- Enrollment_Signature

After successful validation of the Access Key signature by network nodes and verification of other recovery parameters, the information is recorded and stored in the Network Recovery Infrastructure. The network then returns an NRA to the wallet.

This NRA indicates to the wallet that the account's recovery information has been successfully stored in the designated network infrastructure (Recovery Storage).

Second — Recovery Stage

NRA in the Recovery stage acts as the control authorization for the recovery process, which is issued after full validation of the request and returned to the wallet along with ASR_Version, KDF metadata, and key_network.

Steps for the Wallet to Obtain Recovery Information from the Network

In the Recovery process, the wallet first:

- Sends ASR-REC_Display_ID and Recovery_Mode to the network.

Then the network issues a PoW_Challenge.

After solving the PoW, the wallet sends the following to the network:

- PoW_Solution
- ASR-VAR_ID

After performing the necessary checks, including:

- Validation of ASR-REC_Display_ID and matching it with the Recovery record
- Validation of Recovery_Mode and matching it with the Recovery record
- Validity and correctness of PoW
- Validity of ASR-VAR_ID
- Recording necessary values for applying Rate Limit
- Other security policies

If the submitted information is correct and valid and successful, it returns the following to the wallet:

- NRA
- ASR_Version
- KDF_Algorithm
- KDF_Version
- KDF_Parameters
- Derivation_Scheme_Version
- key_network

After receiving this information, the wallet reconstructs the key-derivation path using KDF_Algorithm, KDF_Version, KDF_Parameters, and Derivation_Scheme_Version — which were stored in the Enrollment record. Then, using key_local and key_network, it computes Final_Entropy and reconstructs Master_Seed via Argon2id, completing the account recovery.

### 6. Network Recovery Infrastructure

The Network Recovery Infrastructure is the storage location for the metadata required for account recovery — including key_network, KDF specifications, and other information necessary for reconstructing the key derivation path.

key_network has a special role in this infrastructure: this value is an essential component of computing Final_Entropy — and without it, the reconstruction of Master_Seed and the private key is cryptographically impossible. For this reason, key_network — unlike other metadata — is stored encrypted at rest.

This infrastructure is under the control of the network, and its data store is not a public storage on the blockchain; its information must not be publicly published on-chain.

Record Structure

```
Network Recovery Infrastructure
│
├── Index: (ASR-REC_Display_ID, ASR-VAR_ID)
│
└── Record:
    ├── Account
    ├── ASR_Version
    ├── Recovery_Mode
    ├── ASR-REC_Display_ID
    ├── ASR-VAR_ID
    ├── key_network          (encrypted at rest)
    ├── KDF_Algorithm
    ├── KDF_Version
    ├── KDF_Parameters
    ├── Derivation_Scheme_Version
    ├── Enrollment_Signature
    ├── Signing_Key_ID
    ├── Nonce
    ├── created_at
    └── recovery_status
```

Field Descriptions

- ASR-REC_Display_ID = SHA-512(Canonical_ASR-REC_Display)
- ASR-VAR_ID = SHA-512(Canonical_ASR-VAR)
- ASR_Version specifies the ASR standard version used to create and recover this record.
- Recovery_Mode specifies whether the record corresponds to the 12-number or 24-number mode.
- KDF_Algorithm, KDF_Version, KDF_Parameters, and Derivation_Scheme_Version provide the metadata required to reconstruct the key derivation path — and are returned to the wallet during Recovery.
- The index (ASR-REC_Display_ID, ASR-VAR_ID) allows the Recovery Service to find the corresponding record.
- Enrollment_Signature is the digital signature of the entire Canonical Enrollment message, not merely the signature of key_network.
- Signing_Key_ID is the identifier of the signing key.
- Nonce is the unique value of the Enrollment request to prevent Replay.

Protection of Stored Data

During Enrollment, the wallet independently generates key_network using a CSPRNG, then computes key_local = Final_Entropy XOR key_network and encodes the ASR-REC_Main structure based on key_local. It then sends key_network, along with other required information and Enrollment_Signature, to the Recovery Service.

After receiving and successfully validating the information, the Recovery Service encrypts key_network on the network infrastructure side — prior to storage — using an infrastructure encryption key independent of the account's private key. The remaining fields of the record — including ASR-REC_Display_ID, ASR-VAR_ID, and the KDF metadata — are stored unencrypted, since they either serve as Index fields and must be searchable for efficient lookups, or are considered public information.

This Storage encryption key MUST NOT be derived from the Account Private Key, ASR-REC, ASR-VAR, Account, or any other information recoverable by the user.

The Storage encryption key MUST be maintained and protected by the network's secure key management infrastructure, and MUST NOT be provided to the wallet or the user.

Therefore, key_network is stored encrypted within the Network Recovery Infrastructure, and the wallet is not responsible for encrypting it for Storage.

Retrieval of Stored Data

During Recovery, the wallet first receives the information required to obtain key_network — including ASR-REC_Display and ASR-VAR — from the user. Then, in accordance with ASR architectural principles and network policies, it initiates the Recovery process in stages:

1. The wallet first sends ASR-REC_Display_ID and Recovery_Mode to the Network Recovery Infrastructure.
2. The network issues a PoW_Challenge.
3. After solving the PoW, the wallet sends PoW_Solution and ASR-VAR_ID.

If the network validates the information:

- The Recovery Service finds the corresponding record.
- The Recovery Service decrypts key_network in the secure infrastructure environment using the Storage encryption key.

After that, the network returns the following to the wallet through a secure and authenticated internal communication channel:

- NRA
- ASR_Version
- KDF_Algorithm
- KDF_Version
- KDF_Parameters
- Derivation_Scheme_Version
- key_network

After receiving this information, the wallet reconstructs the key derivation path using KDF_Algorithm, KDF_Version, KDF_Parameters, and Derivation_Scheme_Version. Then, using key_local and key_network, it computes Final_Entropy and reconstructs Master_Seed via Argon2id, completing the Recovery process.

Independence from Device and Wallet

This architecture makes key_network and the recovery metadata independent of the device, prior wallet installation, or wallet storage location. Therefore, if the wallet is deleted, the device is changed, or a new wallet is installed, upon successful authentication of the Recovery process, the new wallet can obtain key_network and other required recovery information from the Network Recovery Infrastructure and complete the recovery process.

### 7. Requirement for Access Keys Signature to Prevent Replay and Fake key_network Registration in Enrollment

To prevent fake key_network registration and Replay attacks, every Enrollment request MUST be sent to the Network Recovery Infrastructure with a valid signature by the account's Access Keys and a unique Nonce.

Access Keys are keys that have access to an account's assets and permissions.

Signed Canonical Message

In the signature message, the wallet MUST sign the following Canonical message:

```
ASR_Enrollment_Message =
    Domain || ASR_Version || Account || Recovery_Mode ||
    ASR-REC_Display_ID || ASR-VAR_ID || key_network ||
    KDF_Algorithm || KDF_Version || KDF_Parameters ||
    Derivation_Scheme_Version || Nonce
```

Enrollment Request Structure

```json
{
  "ASR_Version": "...",
  "Account": "...",
  "Recovery_Mode": 12,
  "ASR-REC_Display_ID": "...",
  "ASR-VAR_ID": "...",
  "key_network": "...",
  "KDF_Algorithm": "Argon2id",
  "KDF_Version": "asr-kdf-v1",
  "KDF_Parameters": {
    "memory_kib": 65536,
    "iterations": 3,
    "parallelism": 4,
    "output_length": 64
  },
  "Derivation_Scheme_Version": "asr-derivation-v1",
  "Nonce": "...",
  "Signing_Key_ID": "...",
  "Signature": "..."
}
```

Recovery Service Checks Before Storage

- Validity of the signature on the Canonical message
- Belonging of the signing key to the Account
- The signature was produced by a valid Access Key.
- The Nonce has not been used before.
- Validation of the structure of ASR-REC_Display_ID, ASR-VAR_ID, and Recovery_Mode.
- Validation of the length and structure of key_network.
- Validation of KDF_Algorithm, KDF_Version, KDF_Parameters, and Derivation_Scheme_Version (in terms of structure and compatibility with permitted ASR versions).
- Store key_network and KDF metadata only after all checks succeed.
- Return NRA to the wallet, indicating successful registration of key_network and metadata in the Network Recovery Infrastructure.

Network Recovery Infrastructure Requirements

Therefore, the following must be observed:

- The Network Recovery Infrastructure MUST NOT store any key_network unless the corresponding Enrollment request has been authenticated with a valid signature from an authorized Access Key.
- Every Enrollment request MUST include a unique Nonce.
- The Recovery Service MUST maintain a record of used Nonces with a specified expiry window. Suggested default: 24 hours.
- Any request containing a duplicate Nonce MUST be rejected.
- key_network MUST be encrypted at rest.

Necessity of Digital Signature in Enrollment

Requiring a digital signature for Enrollment is essential to prevent fake key_network registration.

If the signature were not required, an attacker could generate a fake key_network for an arbitrary account and send it to the Network Recovery Infrastructure, creating a fake record — so that during Recovery, the real user encounters an incorrect key_network and their recovery fails.

This attack can appear as a destructive attack or Denial of Service against the Recovery process.

Therefore, the Enrollment signature ensures that:

- Recovery registration must be approved by an authorized Access Key of the account.
- An attacker without access to an authorized Access Key cannot create a valid Enrollment record.
- The integrity of the Network Recovery Infrastructure data is better preserved.

Enrollment MUST be possible with any valid and authorized Access Key.

Why Signature Does Not Replace Encryption at Rest

Digital signature and encryption have two different functions.

Signature:

```
Authenticity + Authorization + Integrity
```

Encryption at Rest:

```
Confidentiality
```

Therefore, ASR uses both separately.

Digital signature is essential to prevent unauthorized registration of key_network and fake key_network registration for an account, but by itself does not provide confidentiality for key_network in Storage.

Preventing Circular Dependency

The account's private key must not be used as the decryption key for key_network in Storage; because such a design could lead to a circular dependency between account recovery and key_network retrieval.

ASR does not use this design.

Instead:

- The Access Key is used in Enrollment for signing and authorization.
- key_network in Storage is protected by an independent Storage Encryption Key.
- Recovery, after meeting the necessary conditions, receives key_network from the Network Recovery Infrastructure.

Therefore, Signature and Storage Encryption have independent roles.

### 8. Generation of key_network

Step 1 — Generate Final_Entropy

The wallet generates Final_Entropy using a CSPRNG:

```
12-mode → Final_Entropy = 156 bits
24-mode → Final_Entropy = 312 bits
```

These sizes, assuming uniform generation, are considered as nominal entropy capacity.

Step 2 — Generate key_network

The wallet generates an independent key_network of the same length using a CSPRNG:

```
12-mode → key_network = 156 bits
24-mode → key_network = 312 bits
```

Step 3 — Compute key_local

```
key_local = Final_Entropy XOR key_network
```

Step 4 — Encode into ASR-REC_Main

key_local is encoded into the ASR-REC_Main structure.

Result:

ASR-REC_Main contains the necessary information to reconstruct key_local, but without key_network it is not sufficient to fully reconstruct Final_Entropy.

### 9. Role of XOR in the key_network Architecture

XOR is one of the main components of the key_network architecture in ASR.

Enrollment Stage

First, two independent values are generated:

- Final_Entropy with CSPRNG
- key_network with CSPRNG

Then:

```
key_local = Final_Entropy XOR key_network
```

key_local is then used to construct and encode ASR-REC.

Recovery Stage

After the network provides key_network to the wallet through the authorized process:

```
key_local = Decode(ASR-REC_Main)
```

and:

```
Final_Entropy = key_local XOR key_network
```

Important Note

XOR itself is not ASR encryption.

The role of XOR in this architecture is to create a reversible relationship between two independent shares:

```
Final_Entropy
      ↓
XOR key_network
      ↓
key_local
```

and:

```
key_local XOR key_network
      ↓
Final_Entropy
```

because:

```
A XOR K XOR K = A
```

Security implication:

If key_network is not available to the attacker, even possessing key_local is not sufficient to reconstruct Final_Entropy.

### 10. Role of SHA-512

SHA-512 is used in ASR to create one-way identifiers:

```
ASR-REC_Display
      ↓
SHA-512
      ↓
ASR-REC_Display_ID
```

and:

```
ASR-VAR
      ↓
SHA-512
      ↓
ASR-VAR_ID
```

The purpose of this transformation is to create a transferable and comparable identifier, without directly sending ASR-REC and ASR-VAR in the Recovery request.

These identifiers are not secrets, and the main security of ASR does not depend on their secrecy.

### 11. Secure Transfer of key_network

After successful completion of Recovery and issuance of:

```
NRA + ASR_Version + key_network
```

key_network must be transferred securely to the wallet.

Requirements:

- key_network MUST NOT be transferred in plaintext over an insecure channel.
- The transfer MUST be through the same secure channel existing in the TRON infrastructure between the wallet and the network.
- ASR does not mandate a specific new transfer protocol at this level.
- Implementers MUST use the same secure communication infrastructure existing between the wallet and the TRON network.

ASR uses the same existing security layer of TRON and does not define a new transfer protocol.

### 12. Complete Enrollment Process — Sending and Storing Metadata in the Network Recovery Infrastructure

The Enrollment process in ASR consists of the following steps:

Step 1 — Generate Final_Entropy

The wallet generates Final_Entropy using a CSPRNG:

- 12-number mode: Final_Entropy = 156 bits
- 24-number mode: Final_Entropy = 312 bits

Step 2 — Generate key_network

The wallet generates an independent key_network of the same length using a CSPRNG:

- 12-number mode: key_network = 156 bits
- 24-number mode: key_network = 312 bits

Step 3 — Compute key_local

```
key_local = Final_Entropy XOR key_network
```

Step 4 — Encode into ASR-REC_Main

key_local is encoded into the ASR-REC_Main structure.

Step 5 — Select ASR-VAR

The user selects ASR-VAR:

- In 12-number mode: 12 uppercase English letters (A–Z)
- In 24-number mode: 24 uppercase English letters (A–Z)

Step 6 — Execute ASR-CYC and Generate ASR-REC_Display

The wallet executes the ASR-CYC cycles and, based on ASR-VAR and ASR-REC_Main, computes the ASR-REC_Display value.

Step 7 — Compute Identifiers

The wallet computes:

```
ASR-REC_Display_ID = SHA-512(Canonical_ASR-REC_Display)
ASR-VAR_ID = SHA-512(Canonical_ASR-VAR)
```

Step 8 — Generate Nonce

The wallet generates a unique Nonce for this Enrollment request.

Step 9 — Sign the Enrollment Message

The wallet signs the Canonical Enrollment message with an authorized Access Key of the account:

```
ASR_Enrollment_Message =
    Domain || ASR_Version || Account || Recovery_Mode ||
    ASR-REC_Display_ID || ASR-VAR_ID || key_network ||
    KDF_Algorithm || KDF_Version || KDF_Parameters ||
    Derivation_Scheme_Version || Nonce
```

Step 10 — Send to Network

The wallet sends the following information to the network through the secure channel available in the TRON infrastructure:

```json
{
  "ASR_Version": "...",
  "Account": "...",
  "Recovery_Mode": 12,
  "ASR-REC_Display_ID": "...",
  "ASR-VAR_ID": "...",
  "key_network": "...",
  "KDF_Algorithm": "Argon2id",
  "KDF_Version": "asr-kdf-v1",
  "KDF_Parameters": {
    "memory_kib": 65536,
    "iterations": 3,
    "parallelism": 4,
    "output_length": 64
  },
  "Derivation_Scheme_Version": "asr-derivation-v1",
  "Nonce": "...",
  "Signing_Key_ID": "...",
  "Signature": "..."
}
```

Step 11 — Network Validation

The network checks the following:

- Validity of the Access Key signature
- Belonging of the signature to the specified Account
- Validity of Signing_Key_ID
- Nonce not previously used (Replay prevention)
- Validity of the structure of ASR-REC_Display_ID, ASR-VAR_ID, and Recovery_Mode
- Validity of the length and structure of key_network
- Validity of KDF_Algorithm, KDF_Version, KDF_Parameters, and Derivation_Scheme_Version (in terms of structure and compatibility with permitted ASR versions)

Step 12 — Storage

After all checks succeed:

- key_network is encrypted with an independent Storage Encryption Key (belonging to the infrastructure).
- The corresponding record — including key_network (encrypted) and metadata (unencrypted) — is stored in the Network Recovery Infrastructure.

Step 13 — Return NRA

The network returns an NRA to the wallet, indicating successful storage.

Step 14 — Wipe Temporary Sensitive Data

The wallet erases temporary sensitive data such as Final_Entropy and the temporary copy of key_network from memory after successful completion of Enrollment.

Step 15 — Display ASR-REC_Display to User

The wallet displays ASR-REC_Display to the user and emphasizes that ASR-VAR MUST be kept separately.

### 13. Complete Process of Metadata Retrieval and Recovery

The recovery process in ASR includes a PoW layer to prevent widespread abuse and increase the cost of online attacks.

Step 1 — Send Identifier

The wallet sends ASR-REC_Display_ID and Recovery_Mode to the network's Recovery Service.

Important security note: The network MUST NOT determine from this step whether ASR-REC_Display_ID or Recovery_Mode is valid or invalid. The network's behavior MUST be identical in both cases.

Step 2 — Issue PoW Challenge

The network issues a PoW_Challenge.

Requirements:

- PoW_Challenge MUST be bound to the Recovery Session.
- PoW difficulty MUST be adjustable by the network. This adjustability is essential for adapting to network conditions, available computational power, and security policies over time.

Conceptual structure of PoW_Input:

```
PoW_Input =
    Domain ||
    ASR_Version ||
    ASR-REC_Display_ID ||
    Challenge ||
    Session_Nonce
```

This binding prevents reuse of a solved PoW_Solution in a different request.

Step 3 — Send Response and Second Identifier

The wallet solves the Challenge and computes PoW_Solution.
It then sends the result along with ASR-VAR_ID to the network:

```
{ PoW_Solution, ASR-VAR_ID }
```

Note: ASR-REC_Display_ID and Recovery_Mode are not sent again; because they were sent in step 1 and the PoW_Challenge is bound to the same Recovery Session. The network knows the relevant information through the Session.

Step 4 — Verification and Response

The network retrieves all information related to this request through the Recovery Session. A Session is a temporary context created at the start of the request and includes ASR-REC_Display_ID, Recovery_Mode, and PoW_Challenge.

The network checks the following in order:

- Match of ASR-REC_Display_ID (through Session)
- Match of Recovery_Mode (through Session)
- Correctness of PoW_Solution
- Binding of PoW to Recovery Session
- Match of ASR-VAR_ID with the Recovery Record
- Rate Limit status
- Other recovery security policies

If all items are valid, the network returns the following to the wallet:

```
{ NRA, ASR_Version, KDF_Algorithm, KDF_Version, KDF_Parameters, Derivation_Scheme_Version, key_network }
```

If any item is invalid, the network returns only the following message to the wallet:

```
{ Recovery Error }
```

The error MUST NOT reveal any information about which step failed.

Reason for ambiguous error:

If the network announces, for example: "ASR-REC_Display_ID is valid but ASR-VAR_ID is incorrect", the attacker can learn that they correctly guessed and computed ASR-REC_Display_ID, and focus their attack on other parts of recovery.

Therefore, the Recovery Service MUST NOT reveal the failure step.

The Recovery Service MUST NOT reveal which individual recovery component failed.

Step 5 — Reconstruct Derivation Path and Final_Entropy

After successfully receiving a valid response from the network (including key_network, ASR_Version, the 4 KDF fields, and NRA), the wallet:

1. Reconstruct ASR-REC_Main: First, using ASR-REC_Display and ASR-VAR, it processes ASR-VAR again through ASR-CYC cycles and obtains the numeric output of each variable. Then it modularly subtracts the ASR-CYC output from the four-digit value of ASR-REC_Display. This operation is performed for all values, and as a result, the original ASR-REC_Main values that existed before the integration step are reconstructed.

Conceptually:

```
ASR-REC_Main[i] = (ASR-REC_Display[i] − CYC_Output[i]) mod 8192
```

The modular operation is performed in the range 0000–8191; therefore the result always lies in this range and no separate range check is needed.

Therefore, ASR-REC_Display alone cannot be directly converted to ASR-REC_Main, and its reconstruction requires possessing ASR-VAR and correctly executing the ASR-CYC process.

Note: In the current version of the ASR standard, all modular calculations are performed in the range 0000–8191 (13-bit space). Future versions of ASR may define different ranges.

2. Extract key_local: After reconstructing ASR-REC_Main:

```
key_local = Decode(ASR-REC_Main)
```

3. Reconstruct Final_Entropy:

```
Final_Entropy = key_local XOR key_network
```

4. Reconstruct Master_Seed and the private key: Using KDF_Algorithm, KDF_Version, KDF_Parameters, and Derivation_Scheme_Version received from the network, the wallet reconstructs the key derivation path:

```
Salt = ASR-REC_Display_ID
KDF_Input = "ASR-Recovery-v1" || Final_Entropy
Master_Seed = Argon2id(KDF_Input, Salt, KDF_Parameters)
```

Then, using Derivation_Scheme_Version, it derives the private key and account address from Master_Seed.

After successful computation of Master_Seed and the corresponding private key, the recovery process is complete.

### 14. Rate Limiting

The recovery process in the ASR architecture depends on two data types:

- ASR-REC_Display: The numerical recovery data that the user records and keeps in a secure location.
- ASR-VAR: The lexical variable, chosen by the user, that serves as the recovery key and is kept in the user's memory or in a secure location separate from ASR-REC_Display.

Why must the two recovery data types be kept separate from each other?

Due to the ASR design logic. Account recovery depends on possessing both ASR-REC_Display and ASR-VAR, in addition to obtaining authorization from the Network Recovery Infrastructure. Therefore, the Rate Limiting policy in the ASR architecture operates on the same logic.

To recover an account, the user has access to both recovery components. After sending the required information to the Network Recovery Infrastructure and successfully passing the validation stages, the user receives key_network from the network. Then, by combining key_network and key_local via the XOR operator, the user reconstructs Final_Entropy and, using Argon2id, derives Master_Seed and the corresponding private key — thereby completing the account recovery process.

The Rate Limiting logic rests on the assumption that one of the two recovery data types has, for any reason, been disclosed and is in the attacker's possession. In such a situation, the attacker still needs the second data type to complete the recovery process and, to find it, is forced to search or guess.

In the ASR architecture, successful recovery requires possessing both recovery identifiers. Therefore, disclosing only one of them is not sufficient to complete recovery. Given the vastness of the search space and the limit of each attempt block to three failed attempts, along with progressive time restrictions, the attacker's ability to rapidly search for the missing data is drastically reduced — making unauthorized recovery practically very difficult and time-consuming.

Basis for Applying the Policy

At account registration time, the network MUST — in addition to receiving and storing key_network along with the ASR-REC_Display_ID and ASR-VAR_ID identifiers — also receive and store the account number (Account), KDF_Algorithm, KDF_Version, KDF_Parameters, and Derivation_Scheme_Version in the Network Recovery Infrastructure.

The network's Recovery Service maintains an independent counter and rate-limiting history for each ASR-REC_Display_ID and each ASR-VAR_ID. In every failed recovery request — regardless of the validity of either identifier — both identifiers' counters are incremented. This uniform behavior prevents information disclosure through differences in counting patterns and conceals the ability to infer which identifier was valid. This mechanism is designed to counter Oracle attacks.

Once the counter for an identifier reaches three failed attempts, a notification-required event is recorded and, per the "Notification" section, will be sent at the next 12:00 GMT.

Notification to the owner occurs only if at least one of the two identifiers belongs to a real account:

- Valid ASR-REC_Display_ID + invalid ASR-VAR_ID: The network considers this request a probable sign of disclosure of the numerical data ASR-REC_Display and an attempt to guess the lexical data ASR-VAR. The rate-limiting counters for both identifiers are incremented and, after three effective failed attempts, a notification event is recorded for the account associated with that ASR-REC_Display_ID.
- Valid ASR-VAR_ID + invalid ASR-REC_Display_ID: The network considers this request a probable sign of disclosure of ASR-VAR and an attempt to guess the numerical data ASR-REC_Display. The rate-limiting counters for both identifiers are incremented and, after three effective failed attempts, a notification event is recorded for the account associated with that ASR-VAR_ID.
- Both identifiers invalid: The network considers this request a sign of a blind attempt to search for both ASR-VAR and ASR-REC_Display. The rate-limiting counters for both identifiers are incremented, but no notification event is recorded for any account owner — because no real account associated with these identifiers has been identified.

Behavior During Lockout

If either identifier is in a Lockout state, the recovery request MUST be rejected — even if valid identifiers have been submitted. As long as the Lockout of either identifier is active, the network MUST NOT continue the recovery process or release key_network.

Requests received during Lockout MUST NOT be counted as effective failed attempts and MUST NOT cause the active Lockout to be reset, restarted, or extended. After Lockout expires, the next three effective failed attempts will trigger the next time restriction per the progressive policy.

This policy prevents an attacker from using a disclosed identifier and guessing the second identifier to continue the recovery process while the time restriction is active, or to infer additional information from the network's behavior.

Rate limiting MUST be tied to the recovery identifiers and MUST NOT depend solely on the request's IP address, VPN endpoint, Tor, or wallet version. Changes to these characteristics MUST NOT reset or bypass the identifier-based rate-limiting policy.

Through the registration record, the network knows which account number each valid recovery identifier (ASR-REC_Display_ID or ASR-VAR_ID) belongs to. The network MUST send a security alert to the account associated with the valid identifier. This alert informs the account owner of the recovery attempt so they can determine whether the request was initiated by themselves. If the request is unauthorized, the owner can promptly initiate the necessary security measures — including enabling Account Activation Delay and setting the maximum delay period, or transferring assets to a secure account.

Rate Limiting Policy

After three failed attempts for an identifier, a 24-hour time restriction is applied. After this restriction ends, the next three effective failed attempts will double the last applied time restriction:

```
3 effective failed attempts → 24-hour restriction
3 next effective failed attempts → 48-hour restriction
3 next effective failed attempts → 96-hour restriction
3 next effective failed attempts → 192-hour restriction
...
Lockout_Time = Previous_Lockout_Time × 2
```

After a successful account recovery, the counters and rate-limiting states associated with that account's ASR-REC_Display_ID and ASR-VAR_ID SHOULD be reset to zero. This reset MUST NOT affect counters or rate-limiting states belonging to other accounts.

Lockout Mechanism Without a Separate Timer

To implement the rate-limiting policy, there is no need to activate an independent timer for each request or each identifier. Instead of an active countdown, the network uses time recording and comparison at the moment of the next request.

How it works:

- Time and status recording: For each identifier, the network records and maintains the time of the last effective failed attempt and the applied Lockout duration.
- Check at the moment of the next request: Upon receiving each new request, the network compares the current time with the recorded time and the Lockout duration, and based on this comparison determines whether the Lockout is still active or has expired.
- Behavior during Lockout: Requests received during Lockout are rejected without processing, with the message lockout_time_rate_limited. These requests MUST NOT extend, restart, or shorten the active restriction, and MUST NOT be counted as effective failed attempts.
- Upon receiving the next request, the network compares the current time with the Lockout end time to determine its expiry; in that case, the next three effective failed attempts will trigger a new Lockout with twice the previous period's duration — following the progressive policy of 24, 48, 96, 192 hours, and so on.
- The Recovery Service and the wallet's user interface MUST clearly display to the user the remaining time until the restriction ends and the time allowed for the next attempt.

Important conclusion: This design — rather than running multiple parallel timers for each request — relies on time recording and evaluation at the moment of the request. This approach dramatically reduces both computational load and state-management complexity, while ensuring uniform behavior for all requests.

Notification

The network's Recovery Service sends all eligible notifications once per day, at 12:00 GMT, and only if an end-to-end encryption (E2EE) mechanism is available. Notifications created after 12:00 GMT on one day will be sent at 12:00 GMT the next day.

Reason for choosing the 24-hour cycle (centralized sending at 12:00 GMT):

- The rate-limiting policy creates at least a 24-hour restriction after every three failed attempts on an identifier. During this period, it is not possible to send a new request for the same identifier. Therefore, in each 24-hour window, at most one block of three attempts — and consequently at most one notification-required event — occurs.
- Centralized sending of notifications at a fixed daily hour (12:00 GMT) eliminates the possibility of an attacker analyzing notification behavior and prevents the attacker from inferring the validity of their request from the notification timing.

Notification confidentiality requirements:

- Sending a notification is permitted only if an E2EE mechanism is available.
- The notification MUST be sent through an end-to-end encrypted channel, such that only the account owner can receive and decrypt its content.
- The network MUST NOT disclose the occurrence or content of the notification through publicly observable events, public logs, or unauthenticated APIs.
- The notification content MUST NOT specify which of the two identifiers was valid or invalid.

Message content:

- A recovery attempt with one valid datum and one invalid datum has been recorded.
- If this attempt was not initiated by you, please take the necessary security measures immediately.

The message MUST NOT reveal which datum was valid.

Additional Notes

Impossibility of Bypassing the Restriction

Changing the following MUST NOT be able to bypass the main rate-limiting policy:

- ASR-REC_Display_ID
- ASR-VAR_ID
- Recovery_Mode
- IP address
- VPN
- Tor
- Wallet or wallet version

Non-Disclosure of the Failed Component

The Recovery Service MUST NOT disclose which recovery component was invalid. The Recovery Service MUST complete all validation steps to the end and MUST NOT terminate the process in a way that allows the requester to infer which component was invalid.

At the end of the process, regardless of which component was invalid, only a single generic message is returned:

```
{ Recovery Error }
```

Applying Rate Limiting to Non-Existent Records

The Recovery Service MUST apply the rate-limiting policy even to ASR-REC_Display_ID and ASR-VAR_ID values that have no record in the Network Recovery Infrastructure. The network's behavior MUST be identical in both cases — existing record and non-existent record — such that the response, attempt counting, and rate-limiting behavior do not reveal to the requester whether an identifier exists or not.

### 15. ASR Security Principle

Neither of the two components ASR-REC_Display and ASR-VAR alone is sufficient to fully reconstruct ASR-REC_Main and Final_Entropy.

```
ASR-REC_Display + ASR-VAR
            ↓
    ASR-CYC Processing
            ↓
      ASR-REC_Main
            ↓
        key_local
```

```
key_local + key_network
            ↓
      Final_Entropy
```

And key_network is an independent secret protected in the Network Recovery Infrastructure.

Conceptual Process

```
ASR-REC_Display + ASR-VAR
            ↓
    ASR-CYC Processing
            ↓
      ASR-REC_Main
            ↓
        key_local
```

whereas:

```
ASR-REC_Display
        ↓
      SHA-512
        ↓
ASR-REC_Display_ID
```

and:

```
ASR-VAR
    ↓
  SHA-512
    ↓
ASR-VAR_ID
```

Overall Process Diagram

```
                  ┌──────────────────────────────┐
                  │ ASR-REC_Display + ASR-VAR     │
                  └──────────────┬───────────────┘
                                 ↓
                      ASR-CYC Processing
                                 ↓
                          ASR-REC_Main
                                 ↓
                            key_local
                                 │
                                 │  (local — not sent to network)
                                 │
         ┌───────────────────────┘
         │
         │
ASR-REC_Display
         ↓
      SHA-512
         ↓
ASR-REC_Display_ID
         │
         │
ASR-VAR
         ↓
      SHA-512
         ↓
ASR-VAR_ID
         │
         └──────────────┐
                        ↓
                   Recovery IDs
         (ASR-REC_Display_ID + ASR-VAR_ID)
                        ↓
                 Recovery Service
                        ↓
           PoW + Rate Limit + Validation
                        ↓
             NRA + Protected key_network
                        ↓
                 (return to Wallet)
                        ↓
               key_local XOR key_network
                        ↓
                  Final_Entropy
```

Important Note

ASR does not say that key_network prevents guessing ASR-VAR.

The attacker may be able to guess ASR-VAR.

But if the attacker has only one of the two components ASR-REC_Display or ASR-VAR correct, that component alone is not sufficient to complete the account Recovery process; because to obtain ASR-REC_Main, we need both ASR-REC_Display and ASR-VAR and correct execution of ASR-CYC cycles.
Also, to obtain key_local, ASR-REC_Main must first be reconstructed.
And to obtain Final_Entropy, in addition to key_local, obtaining key_network from the network's Recovery infrastructure is also necessary; provided that the required information, including ASR-REC_Display_ID, ASR-VAR_ID, and Recovery_Mode, is sent according to the defined process and the request passes the network security controls, including PoW, Rate Limit, and other validation policies.

### 16. What is Recovery_Mode?

Recovery_Mode is a simple field that specifies which recovery mode the user has chosen:

- 12 → ASR-REC mode with twelve four-digit numbers
- 24 → ASR-REC mode with twenty-four four-digit numbers

Role of Recovery_Mode

1. Enrollment:

At the time of creating and registering ASR, the user chooses one of the 12 or 24 modes. The backup steps continue based on this choice.

During Enrollment, the wallet sends the Recovery_Mode value to the network in addition to the described values. This value is stored in the Network Recovery Infrastructure and is used during Recovery for request validation and enforcement of the Rate Limiting policy.

2. Recovery:

At the beginning of the Recovery process, the wallet sends ASR-REC_Display_ID along with Recovery_Mode to the Recovery Service.

The network recovery service receives and checks the submitted Recovery_Mode as one of the parameters under the Rate Limiting policy and matches it with the Recovery_Mode stored in the record. A mismatch of Recovery_Mode is considered a Failed Recovery Attempt and is recorded in the same unified Rate Limiting counter.

Practical Note: The real user knows whether they wrote down 12 numbers or 24 numbers. But the attacker does not have this information, and this mismatch acts as one of the criteria for Rate Limiting.

Notes

- ASR-REC_Display_ID and ASR-VAR_ID do not inherently encode Recovery_Mode in their SHA-512 value.
- Recovery_Mode is an explicit and independent parameter used in the Recovery process for request validation and enforcement of Rate Limiting.

Ambiguous Error

If Recovery_Mode is invalid or does not match the corresponding record, the Recovery Service must not stop the validation steps midway or reveal the exact reason for failure. In this case, the network returns only the following general message:

```
{ Recovery Error }
```

The error MUST NOT reveal any information about which step or component failed.

Relationship of Recovery_Mode with Rate Limiting

Recovery_Mode is one of the parameters under the Rate Limiting policy in the Recovery process.

For the 12 and 24 modes, no separate counter or quota is created. All failed Recovery requests, regardless of whether their Recovery_Mode is 12 or 24, are placed under a single counter for each Recovery Request.

Therefore, changing Recovery_Mode from 12 to 24 or vice versa should not create a new quota, new counter, or bypass Recovery restrictions.

Also, even if there is no valid record for the provided ASR-REC_Display_ID, the request MUST still be subject to the Rate Limiting policy so that the absence of a record cannot be used as a way to bypass the request rate limit.

According to the Rate Limiting policy in ASR, if Recovery_Mode, ASR-REC_Display_ID, or ASR-VAR_ID is invalid, the validation process MUST continue to the end according to the Anti-Oracle policy, and the network returns only the following message:

```
{ Recovery Error }
```

### 17. Numerical Comparison of Entropy Capacity of BIP-39 and ASR and Its Relation to NIST Reference Security Levels

First, precise definition of NIST security levels

NIST has defined five reference security levels for evaluating the resistance of cryptographic systems against attacks. These levels are defined based on comparing the computational cost of attacks with reference operations in symmetric algorithms, including AES and SHA hash functions.

- Level 1: Difficulty equivalent to key search of a block cipher with a 128-bit key, such as AES-128.
- Level 2: Difficulty equivalent to collision search in a 256-bit hash function, such as SHA-256.
- Level 3: Difficulty equivalent to key search of a block cipher with a 192-bit key, such as AES-192.
- Level 4: Difficulty equivalent to collision search in a 384-bit hash function, such as SHA-384.
- Level 5: Difficulty equivalent to key search of a block cipher with a 256-bit key, such as AES-256.

In this classification, levels 1, 3, and 5 are respectively associated with 128, 192, and 256 bits of classical security. Levels 2 and 4 are respectively related to the collision resistance of SHA-256 and SHA-384, which NIST places in the reference categories of 128 and 192 bits, respectively.

Therefore, level 1 and level 2 both fall within the 128-bit reference range, but the type of computational problem is different:

- Level 1 relates to Key Search.
- Level 2 relates to Collision Search in a hash function.

Also, NIST specifies that security levels 2 and 4 require precise quantum security analysis, and merely having a certain number of entropy bits is not sufficient to attribute a system to these levels. NIST defines these categories in order of increasing reference security strength, but the type of problem and attack model are fundamental in determining the level.

Numerical Comparison of Entropy Capacity

BIP-39 with 12 words of 11 bits and number range 0000 to 2047, totaling 2048 states

- Total space: 2^132
- Checksum: 4 bits
- Initial entropy: 128 bits
- Numerical comparison with NIST: on par with the 128-bit reference threshold of level 1 in terms of entropy capacity

BIP-39 with 24 words of 11 bits and number range 0000 to 2047, totaling 2048 states

- Total space: 2^264
- Checksum: 8 bits
- Initial entropy: 256 bits
- Numerical comparison with NIST: on par with the 256-bit reference threshold of level 5 in terms of entropy capacity

ASR-REC with 12 four-digit 13-bit numbers and number range 0000 to 8191, totaling 8192 states

- Total space: 2^156
- Checksum: none
- Nominal entropy: 156 bits, assuming uniform and independent generation of values
- Numerical comparison with NIST: above the 128-bit threshold and below the 192-bit threshold

ASR-REC with 24 four-digit 13-bit numbers and number range 0000 to 8191, totaling 8192 states

- Total space: 2^312
- Checksum: none
- Nominal entropy: 312 bits, assuming uniform and independent generation of values
- Numerical comparison with NIST: above the 256-bit threshold

In ASR, removing Checksum ensures that no part of the 13-bit capacity of each unit is allocated to a control value; therefore, assuming uniform and independent generation of values, all this capacity can be calculated as nominal entropy capacity.

However, these numbers represent the capacity of the entropy space and by themselves are not equivalent to an official NIST Security Level.

Therefore, in the ideal Grover search model, the 312-bit space reduces to approximately 2^156 oracle evaluations.

This comparison does not mean an official grant of a Security Level from NIST to BIP-39 or ASR. NIST security levels are defined based on the type of cryptographic problem, attack model, computational cost, and security analysis; therefore, the number of entropy bits alone is not sufficient to determine a Security Level. NIST specifically emphasizes the need for precise quantum security analysis for levels 2 and 4.

ASR Resistance to Birthday Attack

The birthday attack is primarily used to find collisions in hash functions. For a hash function with n-bit output, the theoretical complexity of a general collision attack is approximately of order 2^(n/2). For example, NIST places the collision resistance of SHA-256 and SHA-384 in the reference categories of 128 and 192 bits, respectively.

This relationship cannot be directly generalized to the entropy space of ASR-REC, because ASR-REC cannot be considered as a hash function, and the attacker's goal in the ASR recovery process is not to find a collision in a hash function; rather, the goal is to reconstruct Final_Entropy and ultimately complete the recovery process and obtain the account keys.

In the ASR architecture, even if the attacker possesses one of the values ASR-REC_Display or ASR-VAR, they cannot examine the corresponding possible space alone. This process is only possible by possessing both ASR-REC_Display and ASR-VAR, completing the ASR-CYC cycles, and sending ASR-REC_Display_ID and ASR-VAR_ID along with other required variables, and obtaining key_network from the network recovery infrastructure.

Reconstruction of Final_Entropy without access to key_network in the ASR architecture is not possible; because:

```
Final_Entropy = key_local XOR key_network
```

key_network is an independent network share that is protected after Enrollment in the Network Recovery Infrastructure and is provided to the wallet only after the successful completion of the Recovery process, including PoW, identifier validation, and Rate Limiting policies.

Therefore, ASR security against offline attacks is not solely dependent on the size of the ASR-REC space, and the network architecture also adds an independent layer to the recovery process.

Additionally, the progressive Rate Limiting policy and the network's uniform behavior in responding to failed requests limit the ability to perform a very large number of attempts in a short time.

Therefore, the Birthday Attack is not a direct criterion for evaluating ASR-REC security. ASR resistance to widespread recovery attacks should be evaluated based on the entire recovery path, including entropy space, key_network, network validation, Rate Limiting, and other architectural controls.

Why Offline Recovery Is Not Possible in ASR

In the ASR architecture, the recovery process is designed such that ASR-REC_Display and ASR-VAR are two independent and complementary components. Possessing only one of these two components is not sufficient to reconstruct ASR-REC_Main.

If the attacker has only ASR-REC_Display, without ASR-VAR they cannot execute ASR-CYC cycles to process ASR-REC_Display values and obtain ASR-REC_Main.

On the other hand, if the attacker has only ASR-VAR, they do not have ASR-REC_Display and therefore cannot complete the conversion and reconstruction process of ASR-REC_Main.

Therefore, reconstruction of key_local requires possessing both components. The attacker must first possess both ASR-REC_Display and ASR-VAR, then execute ASR-CYC cycles to reach ASR-REC_Main, and finally decode it to reconstruct key_local.

Even after reconstructing key_local, the attacker needs key_network to reach Final_Entropy:

```
Final_Entropy = key_local XOR key_network
```

key_network is independently maintained in the Recovery infrastructure and is not derived from ASR-REC_Display, ASR-VAR, their identifiers, Account, blockchain state, public parameters, or wallet code.

Therefore, complete offline recovery with only part of the ASR data is not possible. To complete the reconstruction, the attacker must, in addition to possessing both ASR-REC_Display and ASR-VAR, also access the network Recovery path to obtain key_network.

Two Independent Boundaries of the ASR Architecture

The ASR architecture has two independent boundaries:

1. Local boundary: Requires simultaneous possession of ASR-REC_Display and ASR-VAR to reconstruct ASR-REC_Main and key_local.
2. Network boundary: Requires successful access to key_network to reconstruct Final_Entropy.

Result of this separation:

- Disclosure of either of the two local components (ASR-REC_Display or ASR-VAR) alone does not lead to disclosure of the entire recovery secret.
- Even simultaneous disclosure of both local components, without access to key_network, is not sufficient to reconstruct Final_Entropy.
- ASR-REC or ASR-VAR alone are not a complete recovery key.
- Local ASR information alone is not sufficient to reconstruct Final_Entropy.

# Account Activation Delay Option

The ASR standard supports an optional feature called "Account Activation Delay." This feature is disabled by default for every account.

The user can, at any time, select a delay period from 1 to 125 days and send and register the activation and the configured delay duration with an Access Keys signature to the Network Recovery Infrastructure. These settings can be changed or disabled for the account at any time.

Wallets MUST provide the owner with the ability to view the current status of the Account Activation Delay feature, the configured delay duration, and to change its settings.

### Periodic Recovery Status Inquiry

To enhance account security, it is recommended that the owner periodically inquire about the status of pending activation requests, in proportion to the configured delay duration, by sending a signed query (Signed Query) to the Network Recovery Infrastructure.

This periodic inquiry helps the owner promptly identify suspicious requests and potential threats and provides an appropriate security response window before the activation delay period for new accounts expires. Periodic inquiry alone does not guarantee asset security; rather, it is a defensive and complementary layer that, alongside other security controls, helps protect the user's assets.

Viewing the Number of Requests

Wallets MUST provide the owner with the ability to view the number of activation requests, the delay period, and the remaining time for each request.

This Query MUST include a valid signature from one of the account's authorized Access Keys and be sent to the Recovery Service via the secure channel available in the TRON infrastructure. The Recovery Service MUST validate the signature and the authorization of the signing key, and only upon successful validation, return the number of pending requests and the remaining time for each to the requesting wallet.

### Purpose of the Feature

The purpose of creating a delay in the activation of new accounts is to create a time window for the owner to react in situations such as disclosure of recovery information, information theft, or other related security threats.

When the owner, through periodic inquiry or by receiving a notification from the Recovery Infrastructure, encounters a new recovery request — which they did not initiate or manage themselves — they identify this message as suspicious activity or a potential attempt by an attacker to gain unauthorized access to the account's assets. In this situation, the delay period gives the owner the opportunity to take necessary protective actions before the configured delay expires and before the recovery process for the new request is completed.

Protective actions available to the owner include:

- Increasing the delay duration up to the maximum allowed (125 days)
- Transferring assets to a secure account
- Changing the account's Owner Key through the network permission mechanism (Permission) — provided that this action invalidates the previous recovery data, and consequently, renders the stolen recovery data (which maps to the old key) unusable. In this case, due to the change in recovery data, the owner MUST back up the account again.

If the delay feature is enabled and a new activation request begins, the Network Recovery Infrastructure MUST send a notification or alert about the start of the new activation request to the previously active wallets associated with the same account.

### Reset Mechanism

Any change in the delay settings — including re-enabling or disabling and changing the delay duration — is only possible by resending the settings along with an Access Keys signature of the account to the Network Recovery Infrastructure.

This change MUST apply to all account activation requests — including requests currently undergoing the delay period.

The Network Recovery Infrastructure MUST apply the new settings as a complete reset (and not as a continuation of previous processes) for all activation requests of the same account. The delay duration is recalculated from the time of successful registration of the new settings, regardless of the time elapsed in the previous period. The new delay period, for all requests, starts from the time the changes are registered, and its end time is calculated based on the new valid duration.

The Network Recovery Infrastructure MUST send and notify the new delay status and time — via notification or message — to the associated active wallets and activation requests that are awaiting the completion of the configured delay and recovery completion.

### Status of the New Account During the Delay Period

The new wallet, during the delay period, due to not receiving key_network and consequently being unable to reconstruct Final_Entropy, cannot reconstruct the account keys through the ASR recovery process. In this state, and without the account keys, the wallet will not be able to sign transactions or transfer the account's assets.

If the delay option is enabled, the Network Recovery Infrastructure MUST, after successful validation of the recovery request, provide the Activation_Resume_Token along with general account information — including:

- Account identifier or public address
- Delay status
- End time of the period

— without disclosing key_network to the wallet.

During this period, the new account is only capable of:

- Viewing the balance and general account information through the public blockchain infrastructure
- Viewing the timer and remaining time until the end of the delay period
- Resending the recovery completion request using the Activation_Resume_Token after the delay period ends, to the Network Recovery Infrastructure, along with the required information and following the defined process.

### Delayed Account Activation Process

1. The wallet initiates the recovery request according to the ASR protocol. In the first step, Recovery_Mode along with ASR-REC_Display_ID is sent to the Network Recovery Infrastructure.

2. The Network Recovery Infrastructure creates a Proof-of-Work challenge (PoW_Challenge) and sends it to the wallet.

3. After solving the Proof-of-Work challenge, the wallet returns the solution (PoW_Solution) along with ASR-VAR_ID to the Network Recovery Infrastructure. The ASR-REC_Display_ID and the Recovery_Mode value MUST also be associated with this request through a valid Recovery Session.

4. The Network Recovery Infrastructure checks the Proof-of-Work response, its connection to the current Recovery Session, the validity of the recovery identifiers, and other necessary security controls. If the recovery request is successfully validated — and the Account Activation Delay is enabled for the account — the Network Recovery Infrastructure identifies the account associated with the recovery information and returns the recovery status response to the wallet without disclosing key_network. This response includes only the account identifier or public address, the delay status, the configured period end time, and the Activation_Resume_Token (to be used for the continuation mechanism at the end of the configured delay period).

5. Upon receiving the account address associated with the recovery request, the wallet displays the balance and general information of that account to the user through the public blockchain infrastructure.

Until the end of the configured delay period, the Network Recovery Infrastructure MUST refrain from delivering key_network to the wallet. Consequently, the wallet cannot compute Final_Entropy and reconstruct the account keys through the ASR recovery process. Without completing the recovery process and reconstructing the account keys, the user or wallet cannot sign transactions or transfer the account's assets.

6. By displaying a countdown of the remaining time until the end of the delay period, the wallet shows the user the allowed time for sending the recovery completion request to the Network Recovery Infrastructure. The start and end times of this period MUST be recorded and controlled in the Network Recovery Infrastructure. Therefore, the wallet's display timer is solely for user information and MUST NOT be the reference for determining the end of the configured delay period.

The Network Recovery Infrastructure MUST send notifications regarding the start, change, or end of the delay period to all active wallets and activation requests associated with the same account — i.e., wallets that have sent an activation request to the network infrastructure. These notifications are for informational purposes, and the execution of security controls MUST NOT depend on their successful sending or receipt.

7. After the delay period ends, the wallet, at the user's request, initiates the recovery completion process again. This request, in the first step, MUST include returning the Activation_Resume_Token (to use the continuation mechanism), along with ASR-REC_Display_ID and Recovery_Mode. After the PoW_Challenge is sent by the Network Recovery Infrastructure, the wallet sends the PoW_Challenge response along with ASR-VAR_ID to the recovery infrastructure, and upon final approval, receives the following from the Network Recovery Infrastructure:

- NRA
- ASR_Version
- KDF_Algorithm
- KDF_Version
- KDF_Parameters
- Derivation_Scheme_Version
- key_network

After receiving this information, the wallet:

- Reconstructs ASR-REC_Main: Using ASR-REC_Display and ASR-VAR received from the user, and executing ASR-CYC cycles, it reconstructs the ASR-REC_Main value.
- Extracts key_local: By decoding ASR-REC_Main.
- Reconstructs Final_Entropy: By combining key_local (local share) and key_network (network share) via the XOR operator.
- Reconstructs Master_Seed and the private key: Using KDF_Algorithm, KDF_Version, KDF_Parameters, and Derivation_Scheme_Version received from the network, it reconstructs the key derivation path and computes Master_Seed via Argon2id.

After successful computation of Master_Seed and the corresponding private key, the account recovery process is complete.

If the owner has validly disabled the delay feature, the wallet, regardless of the Activation_Resume_Token, can send the recovery completion request without waiting for the delay period to end; however, the network MUST still re-execute all necessary validation steps and security controls.

### Confidential Continuation Token (Activation_Resume_Token)

To properly manage activation requests during the delay period, the network must be able to associate the continuation of a request with the same registered process. For this purpose, the use of a confidential token is proposed.

Token Generation and Delivery

When the Account Activation Delay option is active for an account and the wallet has sent all required information for the recovery request to the Network Recovery Infrastructure, if all checks and security controls — for the recovery request — are successful, the Network Recovery Infrastructure MUST provide the wallet with basic information — including the account address, delay status, and remaining time until the end of the delay period — along with a random and confidential token named Activation_Resume_Token.

This token MUST be generated using a cryptographically secure pseudo-random number generator (CSPRNG) with sufficient length and entropy.

The wallet MUST securely protect this token and, after the configured delay period ends, in the first step to complete the recovery process, send the Activation_Resume_Token along with ASR-REC_Display_ID and Recovery_Mode to the Network Recovery Infrastructure, and by continuing the steps, without change, according to the ASR architecture, the wallet and network complete the account recovery.

The Network Recovery Infrastructure MUST securely store a copy of the Activation_Resume_Token in the record corresponding to the same activation request.
Upon receiving the token again from the wallet (after the delay period ends), the network MUST compare the received token with the stored copy.
In case of mismatch, the token MUST be rejected, and the recovery completion request MUST be considered unsuccessful.
After successful use of the token, the network SHOULD invalidate it to prevent Replay.

### Cancellation of Delay Period by Owner

If the delay period is disabled — by the owner — by sending new settings to the Network Recovery Infrastructure, the time restriction is removed.

With the time restriction removed, activation requests can begin the recovery completion process without needing the Activation_Resume_Token and without waiting for the delay period to end. The steps are as follows:

1. The wallet sends Recovery_Mode and ASR-REC_Display_ID to the Network Recovery Infrastructure.

2. The Network Recovery Infrastructure issues a Proof-of-Work challenge (PoW_Challenge) and sends it to the wallet.

3. After solving the challenge, the wallet returns its solution (PoW_Solution) along with ASR-VAR_ID to the Network Recovery Infrastructure.

4. The Network Recovery Infrastructure checks the validity of the request, the correctness of the PoW, and other security controls. Upon successful approval, it sends the following to the wallet:

- NRA
- ASR_Version
- KDF_Algorithm
- KDF_Version
- KDF_Parameters
- Derivation_Scheme_Version
- key_network

5. After receiving this information, the wallet:

- Reconstructs ASR-REC_Main: Using ASR-REC_Display and ASR-VAR received from the user, and executing ASR-CYC cycles.
- Extracts key_local: By decoding ASR-REC_Main.
- Reconstructs Final_Entropy: By combining key_local (local share) and key_network (network share) via the XOR operator.
- Reconstructs Master_Seed and the private key: Using KDF_Algorithm, KDF_Version, KDF_Parameters, and Derivation_Scheme_Version received from the network, it reconstructs the key derivation path and computes Master_Seed via Argon2id.

6. After successful computation of Master_Seed and the private key, account activation is complete.

### Security Considerations

- Enabling or disabling, as well as changing the delay duration, is only permitted with the owner's valid and authorized signature via Access Keys and registering the request in the Network Recovery Infrastructure.
- The reference for the start and end of the delay period for a request is the Network Recovery Infrastructure; therefore, changing the device's clock or date is ineffective in bypassing this time restriction.
- Repeated and multiple recovery requests MUST NOT be able to shorten the configured delay or alter the protective status.
- The end of the delay period alone is not sufficient for delivering key_network. The network infrastructure MUST, in addition to the deadline, re-receive, check, and confirm the status of the activation process and the validity of the final request (including the values ASR-REC_Display_ID and ASR-VAR_ID).
- The start, change, and end of the delay period MUST be recorded in the Network Recovery Infrastructure.
- Notification to other active wallets associated with the account, if eligible wallets exist, SHOULD be sent; however, applying settings and calculating the end time MUST NOT depend on the successful delivery of the notification.

# Responsibilities

### 1. User Responsibilities

The user is responsible for the secure and separate safekeeping of the two recovery components:

- ASR-REC_Display: The numerical recovery data — consisting of 12 or 24 four-digit numbers — that the wallet computes and displays to the user. The user must record these recovery numbers and keep them in a secure location.
- ASR-VAR: The private variable, chosen by the user, that serves as the recovery key; it is kept in the user's memory or in a location separate from ASR-REC_Display.

The user must take care to safeguard both recovery data components:

- If either of the two recovery data components is lost, the user must re-initiate backup.
- If one of the recovery data components is disclosed, the user must promptly initiate the necessary security measures.

ASR-VAR is processed locally during recovery; therefore, neither the wallet nor the Network Recovery Infrastructure stores it.

It is recommended that the user, by enabling the "Account Activation Delay" option, periodically and in proportion to the configured delay duration, inquire about the status of account activation requests from the Network Recovery Infrastructure; and if they identify a request that was not initiated or managed by themselves, they should promptly initiate the necessary security measures.

### 2. Wallet Responsibilities

Backup Mode Selection and Input Validation

When initiating the backup process, the wallet presents both the 12-number and 24-number modes to the user so that the user can choose one. After the user selects the 12-number or 24-number mode, the rest of the generation and recovery process proceeds based on that selection.

Before performing any cryptographic computation, the wallet validates the inputs. For ASR-REC_Display, only English digits in the range 0000 to 8191 and structural conformance to ASCII digits are accepted. For ASR-VAR, only uppercase English letters A to Z with ASCII encoding and at most two consecutive repeated letters are accepted — without lowercase letters, spaces, digits, characters from other languages, symbols, or other Unicode characters. This validation MUST be performed at the moment of input by the wallet.

Account Creation

The wallet performs local cryptographic computations as follows: it first generates Final_Entropy with a CSPRNG — 156 bits in 12-number mode and 312 bits in 24-number mode. It then independently generates key_network with a CSPRNG, of the same length as Final_Entropy, and computes key_local = Final_Entropy XOR key_network. It encodes key_local into the ASR-REC_Main structure, executes the ASR-CYC cycles based on ASR-VAR, and computes ASR-REC_Display from the modular integration of ASR-REC_Main with ASR-CYC_Output in the range 0000 to 8191. It also computes the identifiers using SHA-512:

```
ASR-REC_Display_ID = SHA-512(Canonical_ASR-REC_Display)
ASR-VAR_ID = SHA-512(Canonical_ASR-VAR)
```

Enrollment

The wallet sends the enrollment request — including ASR_Version, Account, Recovery_Mode, ASR-REC_Display_ID, ASR-VAR_ID, key_network, KDF_Algorithm, KDF_Version, KDF_Parameters, Derivation_Scheme_Version (as defined in Section 5.1), Nonce, and signed with an Access Key — to the Network Recovery Infrastructure.

After successful enrollment and receipt of confirmation from the Network Recovery Infrastructure, the wallet MUST erase the temporary copies of key_network and Final_Entropy from memory, and MUST NOT store, display, or in any way provide key_network to the user — because key_network is a recovery key belonging to the network, not a user key. The wallet then displays ASR-REC_Display to the user and emphasizes that ASR-VAR MUST be kept separately.

Recovery Process

In the recovery process, the wallet first receives ASR-REC_Display and ASR-VAR from the user and sends ASR-REC_Display_ID and Recovery_Mode to the Network Recovery Infrastructure. After solving the PoW_Challenge and sending PoW_Solution along with ASR-VAR_ID, and upon successful validation, it receives key_network, ASR_Version, and the four KDF fields from the Network Recovery Infrastructure.

The wallet then executes the ASR-CYC cycles corresponding to the received ASR_Version, reconstructs ASR-REC_Main via modular subtraction of ASR-CYC_Output from ASR-REC_Display in the range 0000 to 8191, decodes ASR-REC_Main to extract key_local, and computes Final_Entropy = key_local XOR key_network. Finally, using KDF_Algorithm, KDF_Version, KDF_Parameters, and Derivation_Scheme_Version, it reconstructs the key derivation path and derives Master_Seed and the private key via Argon2id — thereby completing the account recovery process.

If the Account Activation Delay Option Is Enabled

The wallet displays to the owner the current status of the option, the configured duration, and the ability to change its settings; and, in the periodic inquiry of requests — signed with Access Keys and sent to the Network Recovery Infrastructure — it displays to the owner the number of activation requests and the remaining time until activation of each. It also displays a countdown timer to the user until the end of the configured deadline. The wallet MUST securely protect Activation_Resume_Token in its own memory and, at the end of the delay period and at the user's request, send it along with ASR-REC_Display_ID and Recovery_Mode in the first step of the recovery completion request to the Network Recovery Infrastructure.

Wallet Design Limitation

The wallet is intentionally designed to lack the ability to independently reconstruct the Seed or derive the private key: the wallet is not the owner of the Seed, does not have final control over the account, and cannot complete recovery offline or unilaterally. This limitation is a fundamental security constraint in the ASR design, not an implementation flaw.

### 3. Network Recovery Infrastructure Responsibilities

Safekeeping of key_network and Recovery Metadata

The most important responsibility of this infrastructure is to store key_network in its Storage in encrypted form at-rest (encryption in the at-rest state). The Storage encryption key MUST NOT be derived from the account's private key, ASR-REC, ASR-VAR, Account, or any information recoverable by the user. This key MUST be maintained and protected by the network's secure key management infrastructure, and MUST NOT be provided to the wallet or the user.

During Enrollment

Receiving the enrollment request and storing key_network must be accompanied by a valid signature from the account's Access Keys and a unique Nonce. This request includes ASR-REC_Display_ID, ASR-VAR_ID, Recovery_Mode, key_network, the KDF fields, and the Access Key signature. After validating the Access Key signature, the signature's belonging to the account, the validity of Signing_Key_ID, the previous non-use of the Nonce, and the structure of key_network — and after the success of all checks — the infrastructure stores key_network and returns an NRA (meaning successful registration in the Network Recovery Infrastructure) to the originating wallet.

The Network Recovery Infrastructure MUST refrain from storing key_network if the enrollment request is not accompanied by a valid signature from the account's Access Keys. Additionally, maintaining a record of used Nonces with a specified expiry window (suggested: 24 hours) and rejecting any request containing a duplicate Nonce are among the other responsibilities of this infrastructure.

During Recovery

In the recovery process, after receiving ASR-REC_Display_ID and Recovery_Mode from the wallet, the network does not declare their validity or invalidity at this stage — or at subsequent stages, before completion and throughout all stages. The network's behavior MUST be identical in both cases — receiving correct information and receiving incorrect information.

The network issues PoW_Challenge bound to the Recovery Session and with the ability to adjust PoW difficulty, in proportion to network conditions, available computational power, and security policies — such that the PoW both prevents mass attacks and remains tolerable for legitimate users.

The network MUST execute all validation stages to the end, regardless of which stage identified an invalid component. The network MUST NOT halt the process or provide a response in a manner that allows the requester to determine which component was valid and which was invalid, or to obtain information for narrowing their search space by observing the network's reactions or the progress of validation and the stage at which incorrect information was identified.

Therefore, after passing through all recovery stages, if any stage was submitted with incorrect information, the network's Recovery Service behaves identically and, only at the final stage, in the event of recovery failure, confines itself to sending a single generic message — without disclosing which component was invalid:

```
{ Recovery Error }
```

During Delivery of key_network

After correct information is sent to the Network Recovery Infrastructure and the validation and rate-limiting process is successfully completed, the network returns key_network, ASR_Version, and the four KDF fields along with the NRA to the wallet. The network recovery system MUST use the same secure channel existing in the infrastructure between the wallet and the network for receiving and sending information.

During Rate Limiting

Rate limiting in ASR is designed to prevent adaptive online attacks. The basic assumption is that the attacker has obtained one of the recovery data components and, by altering it, is trying to find the correct data and obtain key_network from the network. The network maintains an independent rate-limiting counter and history for each ASR-REC_Display_ID and each ASR-VAR_ID, and in each failed request — regardless of the validity or invalidity of either identifier — increments the counters of both identifiers. This uniform behavior prevents information disclosure through differences in counting patterns and conceals the ability to infer which identifier was valid; a mechanism designed to counter Oracle attacks.

After three failed attempts for an identifier, a 24-hour time restriction is applied. After this restriction ends, the next three effective failed attempts double the last applied time restriction — following the progressive policy of 24, 48, 96, 192 hours and so on. If either of the two identifiers is in a Lockout state, the recovery request MUST be rejected — even if valid identifiers have been submitted. Requests received during Lockout MUST NOT be counted as effective failed attempts and MUST NOT cause the active Lockout to be reset, restarted, or extended.

To implement this policy, there is no need to activate an independent timer for each request or each identifier. Instead of an active countdown, the network uses time recording and comparison at the moment of the next request: the time of the last effective failed attempt and the applied Lockout duration are recorded, and upon receiving each new request, the network compares the current time with the recorded time and the Lockout duration and determines whether the Lockout is still active or has expired. Requests received during Lockout are rejected without processing, with the message lockout_time_rate_limited. This approach reduces both computational load and state-management complexity and ensures uniform behavior for all requests. The Recovery Service and the wallet's user interface MUST clearly display to the user the remaining time until the restriction ends and the time allowed for the next attempt.

Regarding notifications, the network's Recovery Service sends all eligible notifications once per day, at 12:00 GMT, and only if an end-to-end encryption (E2EE) mechanism is available. The reason for this choice is that if the network were to send a notification to the account immediately upon three failed attempts, the attacker could realize that one of their guesses was correct; therefore, centralized sending of notifications at a fixed daily hour (12:00 GMT) eliminates this possibility. Notifications created after 12:00 GMT on one day will be sent at 12:00 GMT the next day. Notification occurs only if at least one of the two identifiers belongs to a real account, and the message content MUST NOT specify which datum was valid.

If the Account Activation Delay Option Is Enabled

If the Account Activation Delay option is enabled, the Network Recovery Infrastructure performs the following:

- Validating the signature and authorization of the signing key in inquiries regarding the status of activation requests.
- Returning the number of pending requests and the remaining time for each request, only after successful validation with a signature from the account's Access Keys.
- Applying new settings as a complete reset (and not as a continuation of previous processes) for all activation requests of the same account.
- Recalculating the delay duration from the time of successful registration of the new settings, regardless of the time elapsed in the previous period.
- Sending a notification or message regarding the new delay status and time to the associated active wallets.
- Providing Activation_Resume_Token along with general account information — including the account identifier or public address, the delay status, and the end time of the period — to the wallet, without disclosing key_network.
- Refraining from delivering key_network to the wallet until the end of the configured delay period.
- The reference for determining the start and end of the delay period is the Network Recovery Infrastructure — not the counter displayed to the user in the wallet.
- Sending notifications regarding the start, change, or end of the delay period to all active wallets and activation requests associated with the same account.
- Re-executing all validation stages by the network upon receiving the recovery completion request after the delay period ends and upon receiving Activation_Resume_Token.

Access Limitation

The Core network has no access to the raw ASR-VAR and ASR-REC_Display — it only maintains their hashes (ASR-VAR_ID and ASR-REC_Display_ID) as Index. It also does not store or reconstruct the Seed, the private key, or any other recovery data, and does not participate in the wallet's local cryptographic computations.

# Countering Bulk Offline Account Generation in the ASR Architecture through the Mandatory Use of Argon2id KDF

The ASR architecture implements and mandates the Key Derivation Function (KDF) precisely, standardly, and uniformly across all ASR-compatible wallets to achieve the following security objectives:

First — Preventing bulk offline account generation: Given that the account-creation process in blockchain is offline, an attacker can inexpensively generate millions of accounts in an offline environment and simply compare the resulting addresses against the addresses existing on the network. If the address of any of these accounts matches an existing account, the attacker has effectively obtained the private key of that account.

Second — Preventing the accidental generation of similar accounts with identical private keys: Even without any attacker involvement, when a user creates a new account on the blockchain, there is a probability that the new account's private key will be identical to one of the existing accounts — although this probability is extremely low, it is not zero.


ASR, by mandating the use of Argon2id as the default KDF, dramatically increases the cost of each guess. However, Argon2id alone does not prevent bulk generation of random accounts or address collisions. But it renders the generation of millions of accounts economically unjustifiable.

Key point for the user: The user needs to understand that if an identical input (a single random datum) is fed to two different KDFs — one a conventional, insecure-against-bulk-offline-generation KDF (such as PBKDF2), and the other a KDF with high hardware cost (such as Argon2id) — the outputs of these two KDFs will not be identical and will lead to different public and private keys.

Consequently, when a user creates an account using a resistant KDF (Argon2id):

- An attacker using conventional KDFs (PBKDF2) cannot recover the account — even if they possess the input, the PBKDF2 output leads to different keys and therefore a different account.
- For three key reasons, an attacker does not turn to resistant KDFs:
  - First, the high hardware cost.
  - Second, the need for bulk account generation.
  - Third, the vastness of the search space.
  The combination of these three factors makes the attack economically unjustifiable.

In this way, by choosing the ASR architecture — and consequently, the mandate of the Argon2id KDF and higher entropy compared to conventional algorithms, as well as the quality of CSPRNG — the user makes their account more resilient in the long term against two threats: "bulk offline account generation" and "accidental collision."


In many common methods — including BIP-39 — PBKDF2 is used to derive the wallet Seed. In contrast, Argon2id is a memory-hard algorithm that, with configurable parameters for memory, number of passes, and degree of parallelism, can dramatically increase the computational and memory cost of each attempt to guess the input data.

Therefore, ASR mandates that Argon2id be defined as the standard and default KDF in the key-derivation path of this architecture.

### 1. Why ASR Mandates the Use of Argon2id

1.1. Producing Different Keys with Different KDFs

If an identical random datum is fed to two different KDFs — such as PBKDF2 and Argon2id — a different derived output results. More precisely: these two KDFs, due to algorithmic differences, do not produce identical outputs even with identical inputs.

For example, if an account has been created with Argon2id, an attempt to recover the account with PBKDF2 — even using the same input — yields a different derived output. Consequently, the resulting private key, public key, and blockchain address will be different.

Hence, by mandating Argon2id as its default KDF, ASR takes an important step in enhancing the asset security of users' digital accounts.

1.2. Probability of Address Collisions

If the cryptographic entropy is generated correctly, the probability that two independent accounts share the same address is extremely small; but mathematically, it is not zero. This threat has two distinct scenarios:

First scenario — accidental collision between two users: When N independent accounts exist on the network, the probability that at least two accounts from this set share the same address (birthday attack):

```
P ≈ N(N−1) / 2^161
```

Second scenario — an attacker's active attack against the network: When an attacker generates N accounts and the network has M existing accounts, the probability that at least one of the N accounts matches at least one of the M existing accounts:

```
P ≈ N × M / 2^160
```

This formula is mathematically akin to the logic of the "birthday attack" — with the difference that, rather than a collision within a single set, it measures collisions between two sets (the attacker's N accounts and the network's M accounts).

These estimates rest on correct key generation and an approximately uniform distribution of addresses.

Although these probabilities are negligible in practical conditions, the possibility of collision is mathematically not zero. Therefore, ASR considers secure entropy generation, consistency in key derivation, and protection against guessing recovery data — through preventing offline account creation — to be important security considerations.

1.3. Structural Weakness of Conventional Blockchain Systems

In conventional blockchain systems, the wallet generates the private key, computes the corresponding public key and address entirely offline. The network typically verifies signatures and applies consensus rules; but it usually does not know which KDF was used to generate the private key.

As a result, an attacker can generate candidate keypairs and their corresponding addresses offline — without registering any candidate account on the blockchain. If the attacker's goal is to guess the recovery data of a specific account, they can derive candidate inputs and compare the resulting public identifiers against the target account's identifier or the target network.

By obtaining the public identifier and querying the network — given that addresses are public — the attacker learns whether their guess was correct. Then, given the low cost of account creation under PBKDF2, they repeat the process until success is achieved.

The security objective of ASR is to standardize secure key derivation and to dramatically increase the computational and memory cost of each attempt to derive a candidate input in the offline account-creation process, in order to enhance the security of digital accounts. This standard, by mandating a KDF resistant to offline attacks and by making the user aware of the requirement to use the correct default KDF of this architecture, takes important steps to enhance account security and protect users' assets.

The user should know that choosing the correct KDF at account-creation time has three security consequences:

- The probability of an offline creation of an account similar to theirs is minimized.
- Bulk generation of accounts with identical private keys becomes economically unjustifiable for the attacker.
- Their assets are preserved in the long term and the security factor of their account increases.

In this way, the user's awareness of the role of the KDF becomes an operational incentive to choose it correctly — and account security is elevated from a mere technical decision to an informed user choice.

### 2. Comparison of KDFs

Different KDFs differ in their security properties, computational resource requirements, and intended applications.

2.1. PBKDF2

PBKDF2 is a password-based key-derivation function specified in RFC 8018. It repeatedly applies a pseudorandom function — typically HMAC-SHA-256 — according to a specified number of iterations. PBKDF2 does not require much memory — and this very property makes it attractive to an attacker, since they can execute thousands of guesses in parallel at low cost. In contrast, memory-hard KDFs such as Argon2id, through greater computational load, render parallelization economically unjustifiable for the attacker.

PBKDF2 inherently provides no strong memory-hard resistance. Thus, with extremely low cost and in offline and parallel fashion, one can generate many accounts and verify the generated accounts against public blockchain data. PBKDF2, for offline and parallel attacks — from the attacker's perspective — is an ideal tool.

2.2. scrypt

scrypt is a password-based key-derivation function that requires significant memory and computation. This memory requirement can raise the cost of parallel guessing and hardware-specialized attacks.

Its parameters must be chosen carefully to strike a balance among security resistance, implementation compatibility, and the performance constraints of supported devices.

2.3. Argon2id

Argon2id is chosen as the default Key Derivation Function (KDF) of ASR. This algorithm combines data-independent and data-dependent memory access and is designed to increase resistance against side-channel attacks and hardware-specialized attacks.

ASR uses the second recommended configuration of RFC 9106 for Argon2id, though its Salt and output lengths differ from the values recommended by that same profile:

- Memory cost: 64 MiB (memory_kib = 65536)
- Time cost: 3 passes (iterations = 3)
- Parallelism: 4 lanes (parallelism = 4) — i.e., four independent lanes executed in parallel across four processor cores
- Salt length: 64 bytes — equal to ASR-REC_Display_ID (full SHA-512 output)
- Output length: 64 bytes (512 bits)
- Algorithm version: Version 19 (0x13)

KDF Input

The KDF input in ASR is Final_Entropy — not key_local alone:

```
Final_Entropy = key_local XOR key_network
KDF_Input = "ASR-Recovery-v1" || Final_Entropy
```

This definition guarantees a fundamental security requirement: key_network plays an essential role in the key-derivation path. Without receiving key_network from the network recovery infrastructure, Final_Entropy cannot be reconstructed, and consequently Master_Seed — and the corresponding private key — cannot be computed.

This dependency is not merely a procedural constraint that can be bypassed by circumventing network rules; it is rooted in the cryptographic computation itself. Even if the attacker has access to key_local, without key_network, the key-derivation path halts at the very first step — before reaching Master_Seed or the private key.

More precisely: in ASR, the network does not merely issue a recovery "permission"; it is an essential component of the computation itself. This distinction is the primary boundary between ASR and designs that entrust security solely to procedural rules.

Reason for choosing 512 bits for output_length

A 512-bit output directly provides compatibility with the HD derivation chain (BIP-32 or similar) without requiring an intermediate expansion step. Furthermore, a 512-bit output preserves a 256-bit security margin even against Grover-based quantum attacks — since Grover halves the search space (512 ÷ 2 = 256). This security margin is independent of the input entropy (Final_Entropy with 156 or 312 bits) and aligns with NIST Level 5 (256-bit security).

Supplementary note — full coverage of the 312-bit space: The 24-number mode of ASR has 312 bits of entropy (39 bytes). PBKDF2-HMAC-SHA-256 produces a fixed 32-byte output — which is smaller than the 312-bit space and, by the Pigeonhole Principle, results in reduced effective entropy. In contrast, Argon2id both uses BLAKE2b-512 internally (64-byte / 512-bit output) and has an adjustable final output length. ASR explicitly fixes this length at 512 bits so that the 312-bit space of the 24-number mode is fully covered and no compression or forced collision occurs.

The extra cost of this choice in Argon2id is negligible — since the costly part is memory filling, not output generation.

This configuration, using 3 computational passes and 64 MiB of memory, increases the cost of each attempt to derive a key. As a result, executing many offline attempts and bulk-generating accounts will incur higher costs. At the same time, its lower memory requirement enables the use of Argon2id across a broader range of devices — including Android phones with limited memory.

Identifiers and Salt

ASR-REC_Display_ID and ASR-VAR_ID in ASR are computed using SHA-512:

```
ASR-REC_Display_ID = SHA-512(Canonical_ASR-REC_Display)  → 64 bytes
ASR-VAR_ID = SHA-512(Canonical_ASR-VAR)  → 64 bytes
```

Reason for choosing SHA-512 over SHA-256: The 24-number mode of ASR has 312 bits of entropy (39 bytes). The output of SHA-256 (32 bytes / 256 bits) is smaller than this space, and by the Pigeonhole Principle, using it leads to reduced effective entropy and forced collisions. SHA-512 (64 bytes / 512 bits) covers the entire 312-bit entropy and preserves ASR's security claim for the 24-number mode. This change is applied uniformly to ASR-REC_Display_ID, ASR-VAR_ID, and the Salt.

Reason for choosing a Salt derived from ASR-REC_Display_ID:

If ASR-REC_Display_ID is used as the Salt, the wallet can compute this value locally from ASR-REC_Display at recovery time and does not need to receive it from the network.

Using this identifier as the Salt:

- Prevents circular dependency — because the identifier is derived from the recovery data, not from the private key.
- Requires no new field or synchronization — because the wallet reconstructs ASR-REC_Display_ID itself from ASR-REC_Display during recovery.
- Its 64-byte length is compatible with RFC 9106 (the maximum permitted Salt is 2^32 - 1 bytes).

Important note on uniqueness: The actual uniqueness of the KDF is provided by Final_Entropy (with 156 or 312 bits of entropy) — not by the Salt. The Salt plays only the role of preventing precomputation and is inherently a public value. Even in the case of a negligible collision of ASR-REC_Display_ID (probability at most 1/2^256 with SHA-512), since Final_Entropy differs, the KDF output will differ, and the accounts will not be similar.

Two main objectives of choosing Argon2id in ASR:

- First — increasing the cost of offline attacks: Raising the memory and computational cost of each attempt, to reduce the feasibility of performing many low-cost, high-speed attempts.
- Second — creating an independent derivation path for ASR: By using Argon2id instead of PBKDF2, the derivation output for an identical input will be different. Therefore:
  - The user, by choosing the key-generation path matching the ASR default, reaches the primary private and public key for their account — with higher security, of course.
  - In contrast, an attacker, even with an identical input and using the old PBKDF2 path, reaches different public and private keys than the user's account and therefore cannot access the user's account.

All ASR-compatible wallets MUST use this same algorithm, version, and defined parameters so that account derivation and recovery are performed identically. The parameters of this configuration MUST NOT be left to library-specific defaults.

### 3. Economic Defense Against Bulk Offline Account Generation

One of the important features of Argon2id is its memory hardness. In this method, the attacker, for each attempt to test a candidate input, must pay the computational and memory cost determined by the KDF parameters.

This property is ASR's primary defense against the attack of bulk offline account generation:

- The attacker, in an offline environment, generates numerous Final_Entropy values, and for each, computes the private key, public key, and corresponding address.
- They then compare the resulting addresses against the addresses existing on the network. If any match is found, they gain control of that account.
- With PBKDF2: This attack is economically fully justifiable — since PBKDF2 is a fast and cheap KDF, and the attacker can make billions of guesses per second.
- With Argon2id: Each guess requires allocating 64 MiB of memory and considerable computational power. Given the high hardware cost, the need for bulk account generation, and the vast search space, the attacker cannot generate millions of accounts in parallel. These factors render the attack economically unjustifiable.

3.1. The One-Time Cost for a Real User vs. the Bulk Cost for an Attacker

The key point is the difference between the one-time cost for a real user and the bulk cost for an attacker. The real user pays this cost only once, at account creation or recovery — depending on hardware, from several hundred milliseconds to a few seconds — and thereafter incurs no cost in daily use. But an attacker who wants to bulk-generate accounts offline must pay this same cost separately for each guess. When this cost is multiplied across millions or billions of guesses, the attack becomes economically completely unjustifiable.

3.2. The Attacker's Dilemma: The Expensive Path or the Futile Path

A natural question arises here: why doesn't the attacker simply use the old, cheap PBKDF2 and avoid this heavy cost?

The answer lies in the deterministic nature of key-derivation functions. For an identical input, the outputs of Argon2id and PBKDF2 are mathematically different; therefore, the PBKDF2-based derivation path leads to a different private key, public key, and address. Through this cheap path, the attacker cannot reach the keys of an ASR account — because those accounts were created with Argon2id. More precisely: using the old PBKDF2 path, though cheap, does not produce the same keys as the ASR account.

The practical consequence is that the attacker faces a dilemma:

- Either pay the heavy Argon2id cost for each guess — which renders bulk attack economically meaningless;
- Or use the cheap PBKDF2 path — which takes them to a different key space and never reaches the ASR account.

In this way, Argon2id — not by "making the attack impossible," but by closing the cheap path and making the effective path expensive — curbs bulk offline account generation.

This defense is economic in nature and should be honestly presented as such: offline account creation remains theoretically possible, but it is no longer "worth it." The degree of this cost increase depends on the chosen parameters, the power of the attacker's hardware, and the amount of entropy in the secret information.

### 4. The Self-Punishing Consequence for a Dishonest Wallet

A wallet that does not comply with this requirement (for example, uses PBKDF2) accepts two serious consequences:

- It creates an account with a different address that cannot be recovered by any other ASR-compliant wallet.
- The user becomes vulnerable to the attack of bulk offline account generation — because PBKDF2 keeps the guess cost low.

This self-punishing consequence creates security pressure to comply with the standard — without requiring cryptographic verification of the KDF by the network.

### 5. Requirement of Uniform KDF in Account Creation and Recovery

In the ASR architecture, every compatible wallet MUST use the same standard key-derivation method when creating and recovering accounts.

If an account was created with a specific method, its recovery MUST use exactly the same method, parameters, and required inputs to reconstruct the same Seed, private key, and address.

Wallets using the conventional PBKDF2 KDF are obliged to honestly inform the user of the risk to their account and, at registration with the network recovery infrastructure, to send and register the correct information.

5.1. Metadata Registered in the Recovery Infrastructure

During the Enrollment phase, the wallet sends the information required for account recovery, along with a signature from the account's Access Key, to the network recovery infrastructure:

- ASR_Version
- Account
- Recovery_Mode
- ASR-REC_Display_ID
- ASR-VAR_ID
- key_network
- KDF_Algorithm
- KDF_Version
- KDF_Parameters
- Derivation_Scheme_Version
- Nonce
- Signing_Key_ID
- Enrollment_Signature

This information MUST NOT include the private key, the secret Seed, or any information that would allow unauthorized access to the account.

Definition of KDF and derivation-scheme fields

- KDF_Algorithm: The name of the key-derivation algorithm (e.g., Argon2id).
- KDF_Version: The version and exact parameters of the KDF algorithm (e.g., asr-kdf-v1 = Argon2id with 64 MiB memory, 3 passes, 4 parallel lanes, 512-bit output).
- KDF_Parameters: The exact values of the KDF parameters (memory, iterations, parallelism, output length).
- Derivation_Scheme_Version: Specifies the version of the key-derivation scheme — including the domain tag ("ASR-Recovery-v1"), the Salt construction, the transformation of KDF output into Master_Seed, the HD derivation path, and the address-generation method. This field is independent of KDF_Version and allows the derivation scheme to be upgraded without changing the KDF algorithm.

Signed Canonical Message

The Canonical message that the wallet MUST sign with the Access Key is as follows:

```
ASR_Enrollment_Message =
    Domain || ASR_Version || Account || Recovery_Mode ||
    ASR-REC_Display_ID || ASR-VAR_ID || key_network ||
    KDF_Algorithm || KDF_Version || KDF_Parameters ||
    Derivation_Scheme_Version || Nonce
```

Validation and Storage

After successful validation of the Access Key signature by network nodes and verification of other recovery parameters, the information is registered and stored in the Network Recovery Infrastructure. The network then returns an NRA to the wallet.

Honest limitation

Registering this metadata is mandatory for preserving compatibility and selecting the correct recovery method; but by itself it does not prove that the wallet actually used the declared algorithm at account-creation time. This limitation is an honest constraint in ASR's design — not a flaw.

5.2. Verification Responsibility

Account recovery depends on sending correct information to the network recovery infrastructure. During recovery, the user may use a different wallet, and that wallet may operate on its own default KDF (Argon2id). If the information sent (such as KDF_Version) is incorrect to the network recovery infrastructure, recovery will not be performed.

Furthermore, recovery is cryptographically dependent on receiving key_network from the recovery infrastructure — because Final_Entropy = key_local XOR key_network, and without key_network, Final_Entropy and consequently Master_Seed cannot be reconstructed. Therefore, even if all other information is correct, recovery without key_network is mathematically impossible.

This dependency cannot be nullified by circumventing network rules or changing the request path; because key_network is not a "pass permit" that could be forged, but a component of the computation itself, whose absence severs the key-derivation chain at the very first step.

Therefore, the responsibility for verifying the correctness of the sent information rests with the wallet and the user. The wallet MUST send the correct information to the recovery infrastructure, and the user MUST ensure the correctness of their recovery data.

### 6. Proposed Requirements for Implementation

To prevent divergent interpretations, the ASR standard defines the following requirements:

1. KDF Requirement: The use of Argon2id in the key-derivation path defined by the standard is mandatory.

2. Standard Parameters:
ASR uses the second recommended configuration of RFC 9106 for Argon2id, though its Salt and output lengths differ from the values recommended by that same profile:

```
- Memory cost: 64 MiB (memory_kib = 65536)
- Time cost: 3 passes (iterations = 3)
- Parallelism: 4 lanes (parallelism = 4) — i.e., four independent lanes executed in parallel across four processor cores
- Salt length: 64 bytes — equal to ASR-REC_Display_ID (full SHA-512 output)
- Output length: 64 bytes (512 bits)
- Algorithm version: Version 19 (0x13)
```

This profile provides the necessary balance between resistance to offline attacks and feasibility on mobile devices. Implementations MUST use these exact values so that the KDF output is identical across all wallets.

3. Identifier Hash Algorithm: ASR-REC_Display_ID and ASR-VAR_ID MUST be computed using SHA-512:

```
ASR-REC_Display_ID = SHA-512(Canonical_ASR-REC_Display)  → 64 bytes
ASR-VAR_ID = SHA-512(Canonical_ASR-VAR)  → 64 bytes
```

Reason: the entropy of the 24-number mode (312 bits / 39 bytes) exceeds the capacity of SHA-256 (256 bits / 32 bytes). Using SHA-256 causes forced collisions (per the Pigeonhole Principle). SHA-512 covers the entire 312-bit space.

4. Input and Domain Separation: The KDF input MUST be constructed via Final_Entropy — not key_local alone:

```
Final_Entropy = key_local XOR key_network
KDF_Input = "ASR-Recovery-v1" || Final_Entropy
```

This requirement guarantees that, without receiving key_network from the network recovery infrastructure, the derivation of Master_Seed and consequently the reconstruction of the private key are cryptographically impossible.

5. Metadata Registration: The version and parameters required for compatible recovery must be registered in the network recovery infrastructure, without disclosing confidential information.

# Threat Model

The ASR standard is designed to protect the account recovery process against threats related to the disclosure of recovery data — not against full compromise of the user's execution environment. This distinction defines the primary boundary of the ASR threat model: ASR protects the "recovery" path, not the "direct access" path to the private key.

### 1. Threats ASR Is Resilient Against

1.1. Disclosure of One of the Two Recovery Components

In ASR, account recovery depends on two independent components: ASR-REC_Display (the numerical datum) and ASR-VAR (the lexical variable that serves as the recovery key). Disclosure of either component alone is insufficient for recovery:

- Disclosure of ASR-REC_Display alone: Without ASR-VAR, the attacker cannot compute ASR-CYC_Output and therefore cannot reconstruct ASR-REC_Main.
- Disclosure of ASR-VAR alone: Without ASR-REC_Display, the attacker cannot reconstruct ASR-REC_Main, even if they execute ASR-CYC.

1.2. Access to key_network Only Through the Network — With Both Data and Passing Procedural Controls

If one of the recovery data components (ASR-REC_Display or ASR-VAR) is disclosed, the attacker cannot compute key_local — and therefore Final_Entropy cannot be reconstructed without key_network. key_network can only be obtained through the Network Recovery Infrastructure and after passing the validation stages.

However, if both components are disclosed simultaneously, the attacker can compute key_local and send a recovery request through the Network Recovery Infrastructure. In this scenario, ASR makes no claim of "impossibility"; rather, it relies on procedural defenses:

- If the Account Activation Delay option is disabled: After passing the validation and rate-limiting stages, the attacker receives key_network — but this request records a notification-required event, and at the next 12:00 GMT, a security alert is sent to the owner.
- If the Account Activation Delay option is enabled: The Network Recovery Infrastructure postpones the delivery of key_network until the end of the delay period — which the owner has set. During this period, the attacker's wallet has access only to the account's public information (published on the blockchain); and the owner, through periodic inquiry or receipt of a notification, has an opportunity to react.

In both cases, two independent defense layers are activated: rate limiting (which restricts repeated attempts on an identifier via progressive Lockout and increments both identifiers' counters simultaneously, so the attacker cannot infer the validity of their guess from the counting pattern) and time-batched notifications (which are sent at a fixed hour of 12:00 GMT, so the attacker cannot infer the validity or invalidity of their request from the notification timing).

1.3. Resistance Against Offline Brute-Force Attacks and Bulk Account Generation

By mandating Argon2id (64 MiB memory, 3 passes, 4 parallel lanes, 512-bit output), ASR dramatically increases the cost of each guess. To bulk-generate accounts in an offline environment, the attacker must pay a significant memory and CPU cost per guess — and this renders the attack economically unjustifiable. In contrast, PBKDF2 (used in BIP-39) provides no such resistance and enables the generation of billions of accounts at negligible cost.

1.4. Cross-Standard Attack

If an identical input is fed to two different KDFs (PBKDF2 and Argon2id), they do not produce identical outputs, due to algorithmic differences. Consequently, if an account has been created with Argon2id, an attempt to recover it with PBKDF2 — even using the same input — leads to a different private key, public key, and blockchain address.

Therefore, if an attacker reaches a private key via the BIP-39 path (using PBKDF2), that key will not match the private key of an ASR account (created with Argon2id and the domain tag "ASR-Recovery-v1"). The deterministic difference between Argon2id and PBKDF2, together with Domain Separation, ensures that even with identical Final_Entropy, the KDF output — and thus the resulting address — will differ. Therefore, the BIP-39 attack on ASR is mathematically closed.

1.5. Online Guessing of the Missing Component

ASR's identifier-based rate limiting (ASR-REC_Display_ID and ASR-VAR_ID) sharply limits the number of effective attempts by the attacker. The progressive policy (24 → 48 → 96 → 192 hours) together with a unified counter for both identifiers limits the possibility of rapid search for the missing data and, given the large search space, eliminates the practical and economic justification for the attack. Requests during Lockout are rejected without processing, with the code lockout_time_rate_limited, and neither extend nor reset the restriction.

1.6. Oracle Attacks

ASR adopts four principles to prevent information leakage through network behavior:

- Uniform behavior in all cases: The network behaves identically for valid and invalid inputs.
- Generic message: On failure, only { Recovery Error } is returned — without revealing which component was invalid.
- Rate limiting for non-existent records: The rate-limiting policy applies even to identifiers with no record, so the existence or non-existence of an identifier cannot be inferred from network behavior.
- Time-batched notifications: All notifications are sent at 12:00 GMT (not immediately), so the attacker cannot infer the validity of their guess from notification timing.

1.7. Replay Attacks and Fake key_network Registration

Every Enrollment request must be sent with a valid Access Key signature and a unique Nonce. The Network Recovery Infrastructure maintains a record of used Nonces with a specified expiry window (suggested: 24 hours) and rejects any request containing a duplicate Nonce. This mechanism prevents fake key_network registration and Replay attacks.

1.8. Disclosure of key_network at Rest

key_network is stored in the Network Recovery Infrastructure encrypted at-rest. The Storage encryption key is not derived from the account's private key, ASR-REC, ASR-VAR, Account, or any user-recoverable information, and is protected by the network's secure key management infrastructure.

1.9. Side-Channel and Timing Attacks

Argon2id is designed for resistance against side-channel attacks. ASR-CYC — by dispersing entropy across the full range 0000–8191 — reveals no structural information about ASR-REC_Main. Notifications are sent at a fixed hour (12:00 GMT) to prevent inference from timing.

1.10. Phishing, Shoulder Surfing, and Reducing User Error

- ASR display requirements: Uniform formatting with ASR-REC-... and ASR-VAR-..., left-to-right display, ASCII-only, and removal of homoglyph characters — help reduce the risk of phishing and shoulder surfing.
- Reducing user error in entering variables: The mandate of ASR-VAR to uppercase English letters A–Z in ASCII, and English digits in ASR-REC, is a deliberate decision to reduce user error. To appreciate the importance of this decision, suppose ASR-VAR also accepted lowercase letters, digits, and symbols: in that case, the combination of uppercase and lowercase letters, spaces, digits, and symbols — with the many similar characters such as O (uppercase), o (lowercase), 0 (zero), I (uppercase), and l (lowercase), and many more of this kind — would become the primary source of error in reading or entering the variables, and the user would face costly errors during recovery. ASR deliberately restricts ASR-VAR to uppercase letters, and ASR-REC_Display to English digits, and the wallet also validates each variable's input in real time as the user enters it. This combination, in addition to eliminating ambiguity, results in a desirable user experience and, in line with enhancing security and reducing recovery errors, takes an important step toward minimizing mistakes and ensuring a pleasant user experience — by defining a standard input format for each variable.

1.11. Unauthorized Recovery Without Network Authorization

Account recovery without obtaining key_network from the Network Recovery Infrastructure is cryptographically impossible. This dependency is embedded in the structure of the Final_Entropy computation — not in access rules — and therefore cannot be removed or ignored merely by bypassing procedural policies.

1.12. Disclosure of the Seed or Private Key via the Infrastructure

The Core network has no access to the raw ASR-VAR and ASR-REC_Display — it only maintains their hashes (ASR-VAR_ID and ASR-REC_Display_ID) as Index. It also does not store or reconstruct the Seed or private key, and does not participate in the wallet's local cryptographic computations.

1.13. Bypassing Rate Limiting via Changes to Request Characteristics

Changing ASR-REC_Display_ID, ASR-VAR_ID, Recovery_Mode, IP address, VPN, Tor, or wallet version MUST NOT bypass the rate-limiting policy. Rate limiting is tied to recovery identifiers, not to request characteristics.

1.14. Attack via the Account Activation Delay Option

If the Account Activation Delay option is enabled, even if the attacker obtains both recovery data components, due to the lack of access to key_network, they cannot complete the recovery process and reach the private key and the account's assets until the end of the delay period — which the owner has set.

1.15. Timely Detection of Unauthorized Recovery Attempts (Periodic Inquiry)

To enhance account security, it is recommended that the owner periodically inquire about the status of pending activation requests, in proportion to the configured delay duration, by sending a signed query (Signed Query) to the Network Recovery Infrastructure.

Whenever the owner — through periodic inquiry or by receiving a notification from the Recovery Infrastructure — encounters a new recovery request that they did not initiate or manage themselves, they identify this message as suspicious activity or a probable attempt by an attacker to gain unauthorized access to the account's assets. In this situation, the delay period gives the owner the opportunity to immediately initiate the necessary protective measures before the configured delay expires and before the suspicious wallet sends the recovery completion request to the network.

Protective measures available to the owner include:

- Increasing the delay duration up to the maximum allowed (125 days).
- Transferring assets to a secure account.
- Changing the account's Owner Key through the network permission mechanism (Permission) — provided that this action invalidates the previous recovery data, and consequently renders the stolen recovery data (which maps to the old key) unusable. In this case, due to the change in recovery data, the owner MUST back up the account again.

1.16. Quantum Attacks on the Search Space

The entropy of 156 bits (12-number mode) and 312 bits (24-number mode), under a Grover attack — which halves the search space — is reduced to approximately 78 and 156 bits of post-quantum security, respectively:

- 12-number mode (78-bit post-quantum): above NIST Level 1 (64-bit post-quantum).
- 24-number mode (156-bit post-quantum): above NIST Level 5 (128-bit post-quantum).

The 512-bit KDF output ensures that the KDF output is not the limiting factor for security — the limiting factor is the input entropy (Final_Entropy), not the output length. Therefore, the user can obtain a higher post-quantum security margin by selecting the 24-number mode.

### 2. Out of Scope

The following are outside the ASR threat model, and ASR makes no claim of protection against them:

2.1. Direct Theft of the Private Key

- Active malware with access to the wallet's execution environment
- Successful phishing that extracts the Seed or private key from the user
- Physical access to the device at the time of owner signing

ASR protects the "recovery" path, not the "direct access" path to the private key.

2.2. Compromise of the Operating System, Firmware, or Runtime

If the wallet's execution environment is fully under the attacker's control, ASR cannot protect. This limitation is common to all cryptographic standards.

2.3. Non-Cryptographic Attacks

- Physical coercion of the owner
- In-person social engineering
- Deceiving the user into voluntarily disclosing the components

2.4. Weak CSPRNG Implementation

ASR assumes a standard and secure CSPRNG. If the CSPRNG implementation is flawed, the entropy of Final_Entropy is reduced, and ASR's security is weakened. CSPRNG security is the implementer's responsibility.

2.5. Attack on the Network's Key Management Infrastructure

If the Storage encryption key protecting key_network is compromised, key_network is exposed. This is a network-infrastructure-level threat, not a weakness in ASR's design.

2.6. Attack on the Communication Channel Between Wallet and Network

ASR uses the secure channel available in the TRON infrastructure. If this channel is compromised, ASR cannot protect on its own.

2.7. Weak Wallet Implementation

If a wallet stores, displays, or in any way provides key_network to the user, this violates the standard — but ASR cannot prevent it. The self-punishing consequence (inability to recover by other ASR-compliant wallets, alongside the enhancement of the user's asset security) creates an awareness-driven pressure to comply with the standard.

### 3. Conclusion

The ASR threat model rests on three fundamental principles:

1. Separation of the two local components: Disclosure of one does not enable recovery.
2. Cryptographic dependency on key_network: Disclosure of both local components is insufficient for offline recovery — key_network is only obtainable through the network. However, in the event of disclosure of both, the attacker can act through the network path; in which case, procedural controls (rate limiting, delay, alert) come into play.
3. Economic resistance of the KDF: Bulk offline account generation becomes economically unjustifiable.

This threat model honestly declares its own limitations: ASR protects the recovery path, not the user's execution environment. It makes no claim of absolute security; rather, it claims that through multi-layered protection — at three levels: user, wallet, and network — it elevates account security to a degree where attacking it becomes practically and economically unjustifiable. This multi-layered protection includes the separation of recovery data, the use of an offline-recovery-resistant KDF, and the cryptographic dependency of recovery on the network. In addition, by restricting the activation of new accounts when the "Account Activation Delay" option is enabled, and by identifying an activation request that the owner has not managed, the opportunity to respond to an unauthorized activation request is placed in the owner's hands.

# Why ASR Is a Recovery Alternative to BIP-39 (Design Rationale)

BIP-39 is a Seed generation standard that limits recovery to the safekeeping of the Mnemonic data. In this model, recovery is a completely offline, unsupervised process that makes no distinction between the legitimate owner and an attacker.

In contrast, ASR replaces neither the Seed nor the private key. Instead, it transforms the account creation and recovery process into a conditional, multi-stage, network-dependent flow.

In ASR, recovery depends on two independent variables: a numerical datum (ASR-REC_Display), which the user keeps in a secure location, and a lexical variable (ASR-VAR), which serves as the recovery key and is kept in the user's memory or in a location separate from the numerical data. Neither of these two components alone is sufficient for recovery.

The key differences are:

- Two-variable dependency with separate storage: In BIP-39, recovery depends on a single Mnemonic. In ASR, recovery depends on two independent components that must be stored separately — a numerical datum (ASR-REC_Display) and a lexical recovery key (ASR-VAR). Disclosure of either component alone does not enable recovery.
- Structural protection via ASR-CYC: The ASR-CYC cycles operate on the lexical recovery key (ASR-VAR) and progressively spread its entropy across the entire numerical range (0000–8191). Without these cycles, an attacker in possession of ASR-REC_Display could search a small range around the original value and reconstruct ASR-REC_Main. With ASR-CYC, ASR-REC_Display reveals no structural information about ASR-REC_Main — and the search space is expanded to the maximum possible range.
- Recovery data vs. Mnemonic: In BIP-39, full disclosure of the Mnemonic directly leads to loss of assets. In ASR, disclosure of either recovery component (ASR-REC_Display or ASR-VAR) alone does not lead to loss of assets — because Final_Entropy cannot be reconstructed without key_network, and key_network can only be obtained if the user possesses both recovery data components and passes through the validation stages.
- KDF strength and offline attacks: BIP-39 uses PBKDF2 — a non-memory-hard KDF — which, combined with the fully offline nature of recovery, enables offline brute-force attacks and unlimited attempts without rate limiting. ASR addresses this in two ways: (1) by mandating Argon2id with a 64 MiB memory cost, which renders bulk offline brute-force attacks economically unjustifiable; and (2) by making the reconstruction of Final_Entropy cryptographically dependent on key_network — which makes complete offline recovery impossible.
- KDF configurability with recovery safety: ASR mandates Argon2id with specific parameters (64 MiB memory, 3 passes, 4 parallel lanes, 512-bit output) as the default. However, the exact algorithm, version, and KDF parameters used for each account are stored in encrypted form (at-rest) within the Network Recovery Infrastructure. Because different KDFs produce different outputs, this storage ensures that the recovery infrastructure always retains the exact derivation settings for each account — so users need not remember or manage KDF details, and recovery remains possible regardless of which KDF configuration was used.
- Recovery control: BIP-39 has no native concept of "recovery control, suspension, or rejection." ASR turns recovery into an observable, rate-limit-able, and policy-governable event at the network level.
- Single point of failure: BIP-39 reduces recovery to a single point of failure. ASR eliminates this single point of failure by separating ASR-REC_Display, ASR-VAR, and key_network — with the Network Recovery Infrastructure serving as the final cryptographic gate.
- Network awareness: In BIP-39, the network is unaware of the recovery process. In ASR, the Network Recovery Infrastructure is part of the recovery security model — from enrollment and rate limiting, to the cryptographic dependency on key_network.
- Entropy capacity: BIP-39 offers a nominal entropy of 128 bits (12 words) or 256 bits (24 words). ASR offers a nominal entropy of 156 bits (12-number mode) or 312 bits (24-number mode) — a higher security margin, particularly relevant for resistance against quantum search.
- Account Activation Delay option: In BIP-39, the owner or legitimate user has no means of being notified or of imposing any restriction on new accounts activated with their recovery data — and if the recovery phrase is disclosed, the assets become immediately accessible to the attacker. In ASR, by virtue of holding key_network, the Network Recovery Infrastructure can notify the owner whenever a new wallet is added to their account, and — by enabling the "Account Activation Delay" option — can defer the new wallet's access to the private key and assets until the end of the delay period set by the owner. This time window allows the owner to take the necessary protective measures before any unauthorized access occurs.
- Future extensibility: Future versions of ASR may allow users to select KDF difficulty levels — after appropriate testing and validation. Because the chosen KDF settings are recorded per-account in the Network Recovery Infrastructure, such extensions do not compromise recovery; the wallet can always reconstruct the exact derivation path from the stored metadata.

As a result, ASR enhances security not by replacing basic cryptography, but by fundamentally redesigning the concept of creating, safeguarding, and recovering digital assets.
